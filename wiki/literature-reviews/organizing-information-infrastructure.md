## Purpose

Round 1 showed that perceived power drives outcomes — and perception is produced through *channels*. This survey covers how movements built their own information infrastructure when mainstream channels were closed: petition machinery, movement press, church pulpits, symbol systems, and the digital successors. The recurring finding: **the artifact that carries the message is also an organizing tool** — petitions recruit, papers build identity, pulpits coordinate, brands lower replication cost.

## 1. Petition Machinery — the structure test with an artifact (1830s–40s)

The antislavery petition drive is the best-documented early case of information-and-power infrastructure fused:

- **Scale**: 130,000+ petitions in the 1837–38 Congress alone; the American Anti-Slavery Society ran drives from 1834. Petitions were the era's only mass-participation channel — each signature a *legible, countable display of power* (a structure test printed on paper).
- **The gag rule backfire**: the House's 1836 "gag rule" (auto-tabling antislavery petitions unread) was meant to suppress — it *energized* the movement. The suppression became the story: petitioners reframed it as slaveholders attacking every American's right to petition. John Quincy Adams turned the fight into 8 years of parliamentary guerrilla theater until the rule fell in 1844 — a major symbolic defeat for the slave power. **First documented instance of what we now call the Streisand effect, run deliberately.**
- **The hidden output — leadership pipeline**: a dataset of 8,500+ antislavery petitions shows women canvassers gathered 50%+ more signatures than men on identical drives — and Seneca Falls signatories and post-war women's-rights leaders were disproportionately *former canvassers* (4x more likely than men). The petition drive wasn't just messaging; it was the training ground that produced the suffrage generation. Canvassing = the 1-1 relational meeting of the 1830s.

**Mechanic insight:** a petition is a dual artifact — message + measurable headcount + recruiter training. Its suppression can be weaponized *for* you.

## 2. Movement Press — owning the channel

- **Freedom's Journal** (1827, first Black-owned US newspaper): "We wish to plead our own cause" — the founding premise of all movement media: if the dominant press writes you, it writes you wrong.
- **The Liberator** (Garrison, 1831–65) and **North Star** (Douglass, 1847): abolition's distribution backbone — read aloud in churches and barbershops (one copy = many listeners; literacy was never a hard gate because *hearing* was the real channel).
- **Labor press**: hundreds of union papers in the late 19th century; the "outside agitator" frame from the Mohawk Valley playbook was partly a *response* to labor's own effective papers.
- **El Malcriado** (UFW's paper, 1964–76): built farmworker identity and union loyalty *before and between* contracts — the org's voice that kept the community coherent during losses.
- **Samizdat**: Soviet-bloc carbon-copy culture — typing five carbon copies and passing them on was itself the dissident network; the state couldn't seize a distribution method that lived in every typewriter. The act of copying was the participation act — samizdat's genius is that dissemination *is* organizing.
- **Clandestine radio**: ANC's Radio Freedom (broadcast into SA from Zambia/Tanzania), FMLN's Radio Venceremos — diaspora broadcasting as uncensorable channel.

## 3. Meeting Infrastructure — the pre-installed broadcast network

- **The Black church as coordination layer**: the Montgomery bus boycott ran on Sunday pulpits (announcement = broadcast) plus Monday-night mass meetings (coordination + morale + the "everyone sees everyone" solidarity function). SCLC's entire architecture presumed churches as nodes — infrastructure already trusted, already gathering weekly, already funded.
- **IAF house meetings**: Cortes's model uses congregation buildings for meetings — venue + trust + continuity in one package (institutional anchoring from round 1 is *also* an information-infrastructure decision).
- **Taverns and mutual-aid halls**: colonial taverns (Green Dragon — the Sons of Liberty's HQ), fraternal lodges, ethnic mutual-aid halls — every era's movement finds the room where the constituency already gathers.
- **Highlander Folk School** as a node: Rosa Parks trained there weeks before Montgomery — schools/training centers are slow media, producing the people who produce the actions.

## 4. Symbol & Brand Systems — lowering the replication cost

- **The raised fist**: French Revolution → labor → Otpor's clenched fist logo → BLM — a symbol with 200 years of accumulated meaning, free for the taking (Otpor's was explicitly chosen for *stencilability*: reproducible in 3 seconds by anyone).
- **UFW Aztec eagle**: deliberately simple geometry (Chavez wanted something anyone could draw on a flag in a field) — a brand that illiterate workers could reproduce became the union's flag, button, and identity.
- **Silence=Death**: ACT UP's Gran Fury collective was a *design studio inside a movement* — professional-grade propaganda (the pink triangle inversion alone is a masterclass in reclaiming a stigmatizing symbol).
- **Suffrage sashes/colors**: visual uniformity as legible size — a march of identical sashes *shows* the number the petition only counts.

**Mechanic insight:** a movement's brand has a **replication cost** (how hard to reproduce) and a **recognition depth** (how much history it carries). Simple + borrowed-history symbols propagate fastest (fist > manifesto).

## 5. Digital Era — and its trap

- **Indymedia** (WTO Seattle, 1999): first real-time citizen journalism wire — "don't hate the media, become the media" — directly enabled the anti-globalization movement's self-narration.
- **Arab Spring pages**: "We Are All Khaled Said" (Wael Ghonim) mobilized Egypt's 2011 uprising — a Facebook page as movement front end.
- **Tufekci's "tactical freeze"** (*Twitter and Tear Gas*): networked movements scale *attention* faster than *organization* — they hit the streets massive, leaderless, and unable to negotiate or pivot, then collapse or get crushed (Tahrir's aftermath, Gezi, and partially the horizontalist failures of round 1's GLF). **The GLF problem at internet speed**: mobilization without organizing doesn't scale, it spikes.
- **Encrypted coordination**: Signal/Telegram as the modern samizdat — cheap, fast, but the phone is also the surveillance device (see modern counter-playbook).

## 6. The Counter-Infrastructure (brief — detailed in countertactics docs)

Regimes respond to movement media with: jamming (USSR vs. Radio Free Europe), closure (media crackdowns), the "firehose of falsehood" model (RAND-documented: flood channels with high-volume multichannel disinfo — you don't need belief, just confusion), and deplatforming (bank blockades, domain seizures). Modern counters: diaspora broadcasting (Meduza from Latvia), mirror networks, forensic verification teams.

## The Synthesis

Three information-infrastructure properties predict whether a movement controls its own narrative:

1. **Channel ownership** — pulpits/press/encrypted nets you control vs. mainstream media's framing you don't
2. **Replication cost** — can supporters reproduce your message (stencil, forward, petition-signature) with near-zero effort?
3. **Suppression asymmetry** — does shutting you down create a bigger story than you had? (gag rule, samizdat seizures, banned broadcasts all backfired when the movement was ready)

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Channel ownership as org infrastructure
Orgs invest in channels: `pulpit` (borrowed — trusted but terms set by institution), `press` (owned — slow, high credibility), `signal_net` (fast, low credibility, leaky to surveillance), `symbol` (viral but message-poor). Each has reach / credibility / control / cost stats. Campaigns need reach×credibility above a threshold to shift public opinion.

### Mechanic 2: Petition-like artifacts as structure tests
Petition drives, mass sign-ups, signature scrolls produce a *legible power count* — the opponent sees your real number (which is why structure tests work). Dual output: recruitment during the drive (canvassing develops members) + the count itself.

### Mechanic 3: Suppression backfire roll
When power suppresses a channel (gag rule, banned paper, jammed broadcast): if the movement has `frame readiness` (pre-planned narrative about the suppression), the ban converts to a bigger story — opinion shifts harder than the original message would have. If not ready, the channel just dies.

### Mechanic 4: Meeting venues as infrastructure buildings
Churches, taverns, mutual-aid halls = place assets that grant weekly broadcast + trust. Occupying/using borrowed venues is cheap but the venue owner's reputation is implicated (church protects you, but can also moderate your message — the "respectable shield" tradeoff).

### Mechanic 5: Brand propagation
Movement symbols have replication_cost + recognition_depth; low-cost symbols spread virally (stencil, graffiti, song) — tracking `brand_saturation` as a soft power multiplier. Symbol events: a photogenic artifact (tank man moment, the sash line) can spike saturation — but only if channels exist to carry it.
