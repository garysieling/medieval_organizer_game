## Purpose

The user's question: how does faith get co-opted by power — Pharisees under empire, overseers on the plantation, the PMC/intermediary layer generally? The sociological answer: religious functionaries are a **class position, not just a belief position** — they sit at the interface between rulers and ruled, which makes them structurally ambiguous: they can filter power *downward* (control) or *upward* (voice). This survey covers the co-option mechanisms, the intermediary archetypes, and a Foucaultian comparison (disciplinary institutions) that turns out to be the same object viewed from the other side.

## Part 1: The Theory Frame

- **Brokerage (Tilly/network sociology)**: intermediaries connect rulers to ruled — they get power from the gap they bridge. Their loyalty is structurally dual: useful to both sides, trusted fully by neither.
- **Comprador stratum (Fanon, already in KB)**: the intermediary class formed *by* domination — educated enough to run it, excluded enough to resent it. Fanon's warning: the intermediary class inherits the shell of power, not its substance — and can become the counter-elite.
- **Weber's routinization of charisma**: prophecy → priesthood. Charismatic authority can't be employed (prophets answer to God); priests can (they answer to the institution). **Routinization IS the co-option mechanism**: once religious authority has salary, property, and legal status, those assets are leverage.
- **Church–sect theory (Troeltsch → Niebuhr, *Social Sources of Denominationalism* 1929)**: sects form among the "disinherited" as world-rejecting communities; over generations they accommodate → denominations → churches. Niebuhr's thesis: **co-option by institutionalization happens automatically** — respectability is the drift, not the decision. The sect that doesn't die gets hired.
- **Gramsci**: the church as part of *civil society* — the hegemonic apparatus that manufactures consent. Same institution can host counter-hegemony (organic intellectuals): the ambiguity isn't a bug, it's the battleground.
- **Religious economy (Stark)**: established monopolies rot — a state-sanctioned church loses vitality precisely because it doesn't have to compete. Co-option weakens the co-opted (documented across established vs. competitive religious markets).

## Part 2: The Intermediary Archetypes (Jesus's time is a complete typology)

Second Temple Judaism under Rome is a ready-made cast of the five responses to domination — a natural experiment in movement strategy:

| Type | Group | Strategy | Fate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collaborator** | Sadducees | Temple aristocracy; power derived *from* Roman tolerance of their cult | Destroyed with the Temple (70 CE) — power borrowed is power lost |
| **Reformist intermediary** | Pharisees | Popular teachers, Torah interpretation, synagogue network; neither collaborator nor rebel | **Survived** — their network form outlived the Temple → rabbinic Judaism |
| **Prophetic/millenarian** | Jesus movement (early) | Apocalyptic preacher, temple-action | Executed; movement captured/repurposed by empire 300 years later (see Constantine) |
| **Exit** | Essenes/Qumran | Withdrawal — build purity community at the margin | Survived as archive (Dead Sea Scrolls), not as movement |
| **Revolt** | Zealots/Sicarii | Armed resistance, assassination cells | Masada; annihilation |

**The pattern**: the collaborator and the revolt both got destroyed; the *ambiguous intermediary* (organized, networked, neither armed nor captured) is what survived. Networked teaching communities outlast both temples and rebellions.

## Part 3: The Plantation Intermediary Class

### Drivers — Genovese's ambiguous mediator
- The driver (enslaved foreman) was the highest position an enslaved person could hold — and structurally impossible to do cleanly: enforce production targets (serve the master) or shield the gang (betray the master). Documented cases (e.g., Amos Morel at White Hall) show drivers doing *both simultaneously* — mediators, not traitors.
- The traditionalist view (harsh taskmaster, loyal to master) vs. revisionist view (mediator aiding slaves) resolves as: **the role is dual by design** — the planter *needs* a buffer who retains credibility with the workforce; the driver survives by managing both directions.

### The paternalism bargain (Genovese, *Roll Jordan Roll*)
- Planters framed domination as reciprocal patriarchy — "we protect, you labor." The slaves' counter-move: **accept the frame, then weaponize it** — demand the master's obligations be honored, turning ideology into a negotiation lever.
- Generalizable insight: **legitimating ideology is a contested resource, not a cage.** The frame that justifies rule also constrains it — the hegemony has an internal contradiction the organized can exploit (cf. the "reform absorption" countertactic in reverse).

### The double-edge church
- The slave chapel was an instrument of control: chaplains preaching obedience ("slaves obey your masters"), surveillance of gatherings.
- **The same infrastructure ran revolt**: Denmark Vesey organized through Charleston's AME church; Nat Turner was a preacher; the Black church later ran Reconstruction politics and the civil rights movement. Same pews, same network — reversed polarity.
- **The Slave Bible (1807)**: the most literal case of faith-as-information-weapon. British missionaries' "Select Parts of the Holy Bible" for West Indian slaves cut **~90% of the Old Testament and 50% of the New** — removing Exodus (liberation), Psalms (hope in deliverance), Galatians 3:28 (equality), and Revelation (judgment of the mighty), keeping obedience texts. Bishop Porteus's instructions: passages "relating to the slave duties toward the master." Only ~3 copies survive.
- The counter-image: liberation theology's base communities made *reading Exodus* their founding practice — the exact text the power structure feared became the liberation curriculum 150 years later. See [organizing-critical-pedagogy.md](organizing-critical-pedagogy.md).

## Part 4: The Imperial Co-option Toolkit

Documented mechanisms, oldest to newest:

1. **Constantinian settlement (313–380 CE)** — the ur-case: persecuted sect → licensed religion → state church → persecutor in ~70 years. The empire didn't defeat Christianity; it *hired* it. Capture-by-incorporation at civilizational scale.
2. **Caesaropapism → Holy Synod (1721)**: Peter the Great abolished the patriarch and made the church a government *department* — the bureaucratization endpoint.
3. **Ottoman millet system**: religious communities as administrative units — patriarchs as tax-farmers responsible to the Porte. Co-option as governance technology: rule communities *through* their churches.
4. **Patronato real / padroado**: Iberian crowns appointed bishops and licensed missions — the church's personnel pipeline was the empire's HR department.
5. **Indirect rule**: Lugard's emirs and co-opted religious authorities as colonial administration — rule through faith intermediaries at distance.
6. **Mission-educated intermediary production**: the explicit project (Macaulay's "class of persons Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste") — mission schools producing the comprador class. **The co-option backfire**: mission-educated elites (Nkrumah, Mandela, Kenyatta, the Xhosa "intermediary intellectuals") mastered both knowledge systems and *led independence movements*. The intermediary class was trained to run the empire — and did, for the other side.
7. **State churches as controlled opposition**: Deutsche Christen (Nazi-aligned church), the Dutch Reformed Church as apartheid's theological arm, China's Three-Self Patriotic Movement — state-licensed religion that occupies the religious space so independent faith can't. The company-union move in ecclesiastical form.
8. **Colonial communal engineering**: separate electorates for religious communities (Morley–Minto 1909) — *inventing* the religious constituency as divide-and-rule technology; the census category as counter-organizing.

## Part 5: The Foucault Comparison (user-requested)

Foucault's disciplinary analysis is this same object viewed from inside the institution:

- **Sovereign vs. disciplinary power** (*Discipline and Punish*): sovereign power is spectacular and negative — the scaffold, the public execution (cf. the Münster cages, the pillory in sprint-7). Disciplinary power is quiet and *productive* — it doesn't just punish, it manufactures subjects: "docile bodies," soldiers, workers, believers.
- **The three techniques**: hierarchical observation (seeing without being seen), normalizing judgment (measuring against the norm), the examination (observation + judgment fused into a dossier). **Map directly onto church infrastructure**: pulpit = hierarchical observation; catechism/confession = normalizing judgment; the confessional and the inquisitorial register = the original examination/dossier — the Fournier register from the pre-modern doc is *literally* an examination archive.
- **Pastoral power**: Foucault's term for the uniquely Christian precursor of modern discipline — the shepherd who must know *each* sheep individually (confession as total-information tech). Modern surveillance didn't replace religion; it inherited its methods.
- **The carceral continuum**: prison, hospital, asylum, school, barracks — one family of techniques under different justifications. *Madness and Civilization*'s Great Confinement (1656): the Hôpital Général as institution for the surplus/disorderly — confining the unproductive to teach productivity; moral theater as social policy. The workhouse, the poorhouse, the plantation chapel: same diagram, different faith.
- **Panopticon**: power internalized — the prisoner polices himself because he *might* be watched. The ultimate efficiency: surveillance that doesn't need a watcher (cf. the informant-is-the-phone endpoint in the modern playbook doc).

**The synthesis insight**: if disciplinary institutions *produce subjects*, then **movements need counter-institutions that produce counter-subjects** — which is exactly what movements historically built: Highlander Folk School, Citizenship Schools, freedom schools, base ecclesial communities, IAF house meetings. The faith-power interface is contested precisely because *institution-building is how subjects get made* — on both sides. Organizing isn't just mobilizing people; it's building the institutions that make different kinds of people. (This is Freire's entire point — see the pedagogy doc.)

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: The intermediary class NPC
Priests, stewards, foremen, clerks as a `broker` class: dual loyalty stat (power-side vs community-side), power derived from the *gap* they bridge. Players can convert brokers (their local knowledge is high-value — they see both sides); power can activate them (loyalty enforcement, surveillance duties). The Pharisee/driver/priest are one type.

### Mechanic 2: Incorporation offers
Power can offer a movement's faith faction legal status, property, recognition — the Constantinian/millet move. Accepting gives resources + legitimacy but installs `routinization pressure` (the sect→denomination drift stat); refusing keeps purity but forgoes assets. The controlled-church trap: licensed worship can crowd out unlicensed organizing.

### Mechanic 3: Redacted canon / information warfare through scripture
Power can curate what the faithful read — remove liberation texts, emphasize obedience texts (the Slave Bible mechanic). Counter: vernacular/illicit transmission (samizdat scripture, oral tradition, the smuggled Exodus). Literacy infrastructure determines who controls interpretation — the Lollard rule.

### Mechanic 4: The paternalism bargain
Legitimating ideology is a two-sided resource: power uses `paternalist frame` to reduce resistance (protection rhetoric), and organized communities can *hold power to the frame's own terms* — demanding the promised obligations (Genovese's weaponization). Mechanic: the same `legitimacy` stat that protects power also creates enforceable claims.

### Mechanic 5: Disciplinary institutions layer
Schools, hospitals, poorhouses, prisons as `subject-production` buildings — they output citizens/workers with dispositions (obedient, skilled, stigmatized). Player counter-move: build counter-institutions (freedom schools, mutual-aid clinics, movement presses) producing `critical_agency` — the contested terrain is subject-formation itself.

### Mechanic 6: Broker dilemma events
Intermediaries face recurring dual-pressure events: enforce quota vs shield community — resolution affects their standing on both sides, and both power and community can punish the broker. Chronic pressure flips them (mission-educated elite → independence leader is the broker-flip at scale).
