## Purpose

The COINTELPRO doc covered 1956–1971. This covers 2000–present: the counter-organizing toolkit has **evolved from covert state programs to legalized, privatized, and internationalized repression**. Three structural shifts dominate:

1. **Privatization** — corporations now buy counter-insurgency as a service (TigerSwan) instead of relying on FBI
2. **Legalization** — repression moved from covert programs to statute books (foreign-agent laws, anti-protest statutes, RICO) — the tactics no longer need hiding
3. **Digitalization** — surveillance is purchased by the seat-license, not run by field agents

## Tactic Inventory

### A. Foreign-agent / transparency-law repression (the signature modern tool)
- **Archetype**: Russia's 2012 "foreign agents" law — passed months after mass protests against Putin's return. Mechanism: label NGOs receiving foreign funds as "foreign agents" → onerous reporting → fines → closure → stigmatized survivors. Decimated independent civil society (human rights, anti-corruption, polling orgs).
- **Proliferation**: near-copies in Georgia (2023 attempt, passed 2024), Kyrgyzstan (2024), Hungary (2017 — struck down by ECJ, reworked as "sovereignty protection" law 2023), Slovakia, plus versions debated worldwide. It's an **exported playbook** — the mirror image of CANVAS exporting Otpor.
- **Why it works**: it never bans organizing — it makes organizing *administratively unaffordable and socially suspect*. "Outside agitator" got a statute number. Selective enforcement does the rest (unpredictability is the feature: everyone must comply; only enemies get audited).
- **Movement counter**: domestic-funding pivots, diaspora relocation, legal challenges (ECJ), mass protest (Georgia 2023 forced withdrawal — then it returned in 2024).

### B. Privatized counter-insurgency (TigerSwan @ Standing Rock — the fullest documented case)
Leaked internal docs (100+ leaked + 1,000+ via records requests, reported by The Intercept/Grist, 2017):
- Energy Transfer Partners hired TigerSwan (ex-Special Forces firm) after the dog-attack PR crisis Sept 2016
- **Tactics**: infiltrated protest camps; social-media monitoring; aerial surveillance; radio eavesdropping; "persons of interest" dossiers shared with client and police across 5 states; **fake reporters** planted in camps; propaganda campaigns; facilitated evidence-sharing systems with law enforcement; procured radios and less-lethal weapons for police
- **The frame trick**: internal documents relabeled water protectors an "**ideologically driven insurgency**" and "jihadists" — the counterterror framing unlocked military doctrine against civilians and *justified* the toolkit
- **Accountability**: operated unlicensed for months; North Dakota's security board sought $2M, settled for **$175k** — the cost of a full private counter-movement operation against a national movement was less than a rounding error on a $3.8B project. Repression as line item.
- Earlier precedent: Stratfor monitored activists for corporations (Dow/Bhopal, Coca-Cola/PETA — documented in leaked GI Files).

### C. Lawfare (repression via courts, now overt)
- **Anti-protest statutes**: post-2016, 45+ US states introduced anti-protest bills; several enacted "critical infrastructure" felony laws (OK, SD, LA...) effectively criminalizing pipeline protest — written *with* industry (ALEC model bills documented).
- **UK**: Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022 + Public Order Act 2023 — criminalized "locking on" and "serious disruption" (vagueness as a feature — chilling effect on legal protest).
- **RICO/terrorism charges**: Atlanta "Cop City" (2023–) — prosecutors used **domestic terrorism** charges and RICO against protesters *and the bail fund itself*; unprecedented escalation of criminal-law tools against the movement's *infrastructure*, not just its street actions.
- **SLAPP counter-attack**: Energy Transfer v. Greenpeace — a $660M+ verdict (2025, ND jury) against Greenpeace for its Standing Rock role; regardless of appeal outcome, the suit demonstrates that *winning* can still get your org sued into oblivion — counter-organizing via civil damages.
- **Mechanism shift**: COINTELPRO hid; lawfare *advertises*. The process is the punishment — years of legal cost, discovery fishing, and donor chilling regardless of outcome.

### D. Surveillance-industrial tools
- **Mercenary spyware**: NSO Pegasus (zero-click) — Citizen Lab/Forbidden Stories documented infections/targeting of journalists and activists in dozens of countries (incl. Russian/Belarusian opposition figures *inside Europe*). 50,000+ numbers on the leaked target list.
- **Mass/location surveillance**: facial recognition at protests (documented in HK, Moscow, US Clearview AI), geofence warrants, IMSI-catchers/Stingray, social-media monitoring at scale (Dataminr-police contracts documented).
- **What changed**: 1971 infiltration needed humans embedded for years (expensive, risky, limited scale); 2025 surveillance is a subscription — the informant is the phone.
- **Counters that emerged**: Signal/encryption as default, security culture 2.0, and *forensic counter-intelligence* — Citizen Lab and Amnesty's Security Lab exist to catch states' tools; litigation (Meta v. NSO — WhatsApp won; Apple v. NSO) raised vendor costs.

### E. Platform/information warfare
- Coordinated inauthentic behavior (state and corporate troll networks — documented by platform transparency reports and DFRLab), astroturf at industrial scale, targeted harassment mobs tolerated by states, disinfo seeding to split movements (2016-era IRA operations targeted *both* sides of US protest movements simultaneously — divide-and-rule via social media).
- The modern snitch-jacket: forged screenshots, leaked-fake DMs, planted "informant" accusations in movement Slack/Discords.

### F. The administrative strangle
- Permit regimes, insurance requirements, protest "free speech zones," event-cost shifting to organizers, visa denials for international solidarity, banking/fintech de-platforming of movement orgs (documented for bail funds, Wikileaks 2010 banking blockade), IRS/charity-status threats.

## The Deep Structural Changes (vs. COINTELPRO era)

| 1971 | 2025 |
|---|---|
| Covert program (hiding required) | Legal statutes + private contractors (deniable AND lawful) |
| Field agents embedded for years | Spyware subscriptions + data brokers |
| One state actor | State + corporation + private security + platform |
| Exposure ended the program (Media, PA) | Exposure changes the letterhead, not the practice |
| Domestic | Exported playbooks (foreign-agent laws; CANVAS counter-doctrine) |
| Movement counter: FOIA, break-ins | Counter: encryption, forensics, litigation, jurisdiction-hopping |

**The single biggest shift**: repression went from *program* (a thing a state does, which can be exposed and ended) to *market* (a service anyone with money can buy, which can only be regulated). TigerSwan's $175k fine is the proof — the penalty is priced into the business model.

## Counter-Adaptations Observed (2000–present)

1. **Encryption-by-default** (Signal etc.) — raising surveillance cost, not eliminating it
2. **Forensic counter-intelligence** (Citizen Lab, Amnesty Security Lab) — exposing the tools raises vendor cost; NSO now sanctioned/blacklisted
3. **Jurisdictional arbitrage** — register in friendly countries, diaspora media (Meduza→Latvia model)
4. **Domestic-funding pivots** — cutting the foreign-agent-law leverage at its root
5. **Decentralized/branded movements** (no leader to subpoena — though this trades away organizational depth; the Hong Kong "be water" lesson: adaptive but unanchored)
6. **Litigation capacity as core org function** — legal defense is now offense defense (NLG→climate-law NGOs)
7. **Pre-bunking/verification norms** — rapid debunk teams against planted media

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Repression as market, not just state action
Add **counter-organizing vendors** as actors: power-holders can *purchase* repression services (private security, PR firms, law firms) — deniable, effective, and immune to the state's legitimacy costs. Cost asymmetry is the mechanic: their services are cheap; your defense is expensive. Fines are just a cost of doing business.

### Mechanic 2: Legalization track
Power-holders can shift tactics from *covert* (exposure risk) to *legislated* (slow to pass, hard to fight once law). Player counters move from exposure tactics to *blocking the statute* (lobbying, pillar pressure, constitutional challenge) — different counter for different phase.

### Mechanic 3: Administrative attrition
Reporting requirements, audits, permit denials as damage-over-time on org capacity — the foreign-agent-law mechanic. Orgs must spend actions on compliance or risk closure; the choice itself is the weapon.

### Mechanic 4: Digital surveillance asymmetry
Surveillance is now ambient and cheap: every org member's comms leak intel at a base rate (reduced by `security_culture` investment). The 1971-style infiltrator becomes a rare deep asset; the common case is bulk collection. Counter-investment: encryption practices, forensics (chance to detect and *expose* surveillance → legitimacy hit for power).

### Mechanic 5: Lawfare events
Post-win or mid-campaign: SLAPP suit, RICO charge, injunction. Damage is the *process*: resources drained, leaders chilled, donors scared — even if you win the case. Defense = legal infrastructure stat + solidarity funding. The Greenpeace verdict makes "winning the case and losing the org" a real outcome branch.
