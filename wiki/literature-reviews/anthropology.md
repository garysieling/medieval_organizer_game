## Summary

Cultural anthropology studies how human societies organize themselves through kinship, ritual, and cultural transmission. Kinship systems (patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral) form the backbone of social organization, regulating marriage, inheritance, and political alliances. Rituals mark life transitions through van Gennep's three-phase structure (separation, liminality, reincorporation), creating social cohesion and transmitting cultural values. Medieval Europe featured complex kinship patterns shaped by Church marriage laws and strategic alliances. This research provides rich mechanics for family systems, rites of passage, cultural inheritance, and symbolic objects.

## Key Sources

### Kinship Systems: Descent and Social Organization
**Rating:** A+
**Type:** Anthropological Theory / Cross-Cultural Research
**Relevance:** High - Core social structure mechanics

**Key Findings:**

**Definition:**
- Kinship = system of social organization based on real or putative family ties
- Extends to non-biological relationships (adoption, godparenting, affinity)
- Regulates marriage, inheritance, political alliances, resource distribution

**Three Primary Descent Systems:**

1. **Patrilineal:**
   - Descent traced through father's lineage
   - Inheritance passes through male line
   - Women marry into husband's family
   - Most common globally (especially in agricultural societies)

2. **Matrilineal:**
   - Descent traced through mother's lineage
   - Inheritance passes through female line
   - Often associated with matrilocal residence (husband moves to wife's family)
   - Less common, but significant examples exist

3. **Bilateral (Cognatic):**
   - Descent reckoned through both maternal and paternal lines
   - Inheritance can come from either side
   - More flexible, allows broader kinship networks
   - Common in Western Europe by late medieval period

**Social Functions:**
- **Identity:** Determines group membership and social status
- **Political Organization:** Kinship provided framework for alliances, military organization, dispute resolution without centralized state
- **Economic:** Regulates property, inheritance, labor cooperation
- **Marriage Rules:** Defines who can marry whom (exogamy/endogamy)

**Source Quality:** Foundational anthropological concept, cross-cultural data
**Game Application:** Family tree system with inheritance rules and alliance mechanics

### Medieval Kinship, Marriage, and Family Structure
**Rating:** A
**Type:** Historical Research / Medieval Studies
**Relevance:** High - Medieval setting accuracy

**Key Findings:**

**Household Composition:**
- Extended beyond nuclear family
- Could include: grandparents, widowed kin, godchildren, servants, wet nurses, apprentices, lodgers
- Patriarchal structure: father as household head
- Social class determined family patterns

**Three Types of Medieval Kinship:**

1. **Consanguinei (Blood Kin):**
   - Shared common ancestors
   - Church expanded incest taboo to sixth cousins
   - Complex calculations for permissible marriages

2. **Spiritual Kinship (Godparents):**
   - Created through baptism
   - Serious obligations (educate godchild if parents die)
   - Forbidden to marry godchild or godchild's parents

3. **Affines (Marriage Kin):**
   - Two kindreds tied together through marriage
   - Alliance between families, not just individuals

**Church Influence on Marriage:**
- Early Middle Ages: Church obsessed with incest prevention
- Promoted marriage "by choice" (consent of both parties)
- Required newly married couples to set up independent households (nuclear family emphasis)
- Marriage became a sacrament (indissoluble)

**Class Variations:**
- **Nobility:** Political marriages, dynastic concerns, heavily negotiated, primogeniture (eldest son inherits)
- **Peasants:** More local, practical concerns, partible inheritance (divide among children)
- **Urban:** Guild marriages, trade alliances, dowries

**Source Quality:** Academic historical research, archaeological evidence
**Game Application:** Complex family trees, marriage negotiations, inheritance conflicts

### Rites of Passage: Van Gennep & Turner
**Rating:** A+
**Type:** Anthropological Theory / Classic Ethnography
**Relevance:** High - Life transition mechanics

**Key Findings:**

**Arnold van Gennep (1909):**
- Noticed birth, puberty, marriage, death specially commemorated in every culture
- Identified universal three-phase structure

**Three Stages:**

1. **Separation (Preliminal):**
   - Ritual subject detaches from old status
   - Symbolic death of former self
   - Examples: Leaving home, removing old clothes, physical seclusion

2. **Transition (Liminal - "Threshold"):**
   - From Latin *limen* = boundary/threshold
   - **"Betwixt and between"** - neither old nor new status
   - Ambiguous, dangerous, transformative state
   - Subject often treated as socially invisible
   - Victor Turner expanded: liminality = potential for transformation

3. **Reincorporation (Postliminal):**
   - Subject installs in new status
   - Inwardly transformed, outwardly changed
   - Social recognition of new role
   - Celebratory feast, new clothing, new rights/responsibilities

**Universal Rites:**
- **Birth:** Naming ceremonies, baptism, presentation to community
- **Puberty:** Coming-of-age rituals, initiation into adulthood
- **Marriage:** Betrothal, wedding ceremony, consummation
- **Death:** Funerals, mourning period, ancestor veneration

**Medieval Examples:**
- **Knighting:** Vigil (separation), dubbing ceremony (liminal), feast (reincorporation)
- **Ordination:** Novitiate (separation), vows (liminal), full monk status (reincorporation)
- **Marriage:** Betrothal (separation from birth family), wedding (liminal), consummation/feast (reincorporation)

**Source Quality:** Classic anthropological theory, cross-culturally validated
**Game Application:** Life stage transitions with ritual requirements and status changes

**Type:** Anthropological Theory / Ritual Studies
**Relevance:** High - Event and ceremony mechanics

**Key Findings:**

**Definition:**
- Rituals = formalized activities following sequence of actions imbued with symbolic meanings
- **Distinction from routine:** Ritual is elevated, ceremonial, symbolically significant
- What matters is symbolic meaning beyond the action itself

**Social Functions:**

1. **Social Cohesion:**
   - Brings people together
   - Creates sense of belonging
   - Collective effervescence (Durkheim)

2. **Cultural Transmission:**
   - Preserves tradition
   - Passes knowledge across generations
   - Teaches cultural values through participation

3. **Marking Transitions:**
   - Signals changes in status
   - Makes abstract changes visible
   - Gives community shared understanding

4. **Emotional Intensity:**
   - Many people participating together creates powerful experience
   - Reinforces commitment to group
   - Memorable, affects behavior

**Theoretical Approaches:**

**Structural-Functionalism:**
- Rituals maintain social order
- Reinforce norms and values
- Integrate individuals into collective

**Symbolic Anthropology:**
- Interpret meanings rituals convey
- Reflect society's beliefs and ideologies
- Symbols communicate deeper truths

**Medieval Rituals:**
- Religious calendar (Mass, saints' days, Easter, Christmas)
- Harvest festivals (Lammas, Michaelmas)
- Guild ceremonies (apprentice → journeyman → master)
- Feudal rituals (homage, fealty oaths)

**Source Quality:** Established ritual studies theory

### Cultural Transmission and Evolution
**Rating:** A
**Type:** Evolutionary Anthropology / Cultural Evolution Theory
**Relevance:** Medium-High - Cultural inheritance mechanics

**Key Findings:**

**Definition:**
- Cultural evolution = how culture changes over time through social transmission
- Culture = aspects of thought, speech, behavior, artifacts that can be learned and transmitted socially

**Transmission Mechanisms:**

1. **Vertical Transmission:**
   - Parents to children
   - Most conservative (preserves tradition)
   - Slow change

2. **Horizontal Transmission:**
   - Peers, same generation
   - Faster adoption of innovations
   - Fads, fashions

3. **Oblique Transmission:**
   - Elders, teachers, non-parent adults to youth
   - Institutionalized knowledge (schools, apprenticeships)
   - Medium conservatism

**Key Differences from Biological Evolution:**
- **Speed:** Cultural evolution much faster
- **Sources:** Can learn from anyone, not just parents
- **Direction:** Can "un-learn" or abandon traits
- **Combination:** Can blend traits from multiple sources

**Social Learning:**
- Teaching, imitation, language
- Norms, beliefs, preferences transmitted socially
- Shaped by both heritable traits and cultural context

**Medieval Examples:**
- Craft skills passed via apprenticeship (oblique)
- Religious beliefs via Church teaching (oblique)
- Farming techniques from parents (vertical)
- Fashion, slang from peers (horizontal)

**Source Quality:** Interdisciplinary (anthropology, psychology, economics)
**Game Application:** Skill inheritance system with multiple transmission pathways

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Kinship and Descent System
**Academic Basis:** Anthropological kinship theory, medieval family structures

**Translation:**
- **Core System:**
  - Every NPC has kinship network
  - Three types: consanguineal (blood), spiritual (godparents), affinal (marriage)
  - Descent determines inheritance, alliances, obligations

- **Descent Types (Player/Village Choice):**

  **Patrilineal:**
  - Inheritance through father's line
  - Women marry into husband's family
  - Strong male lineages
  - **Benefit:** Clear succession, strong alliances via marriage
  - **Drawback:** Women lose birth family ties

  **Matrilineal:**
  - Inheritance through mother's line
  - Often matrilocal residence
  - **Benefit:** Women maintain family ties, uncertain paternity less issue
  - **Drawback:** Less common, may conflict with Church norms

  **Bilateral:**
  - Inheritance from both lines
  - Flexible kinship networks
  - **Benefit:** Broader support network, more inheritance opportunities
  - **Drawback:** More complex, potential for inheritance disputes

- **Implementation:**
  ```rust
  struct Kinship {
      consanguineal: Vec<(PersonId, Relation, Degree)>,  // blood kin
      spiritual: Vec<(PersonId, GodparentRole)>,  // godparents/godchildren
      affinal: Vec<(PersonId, MarriageLink)>,  // in-laws
  }

  enum DescentType {
      Patrilineal,
      Matrilineal,
      Bilateral,
  }

  enum Relation {
      Parent,
      Child,
      Sibling,
      Cousin(u8),  // degree: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, etc.
      Grandparent,
      Grandchild,
  }

  fn calculate_inheritance(deceased: &Person, system: DescentType) -> Vec<(PersonId, f32)> {
      match system {
          DescentType::Patrilineal => {
              // Eldest son gets majority (primogeniture)
              let sons = deceased.children.filter(|c| c.is_male());
              if let Some(eldest) = sons.first() {
                  vec![(eldest.id, 0.75), ...other_sons_split(0.25)]
              } else {
                  // No sons, reverts to lord or distributed to brothers
                  vec![]
              }
          },
          DescentType::Matrilineal => {
              // Through mother's line
              let daughters = deceased.children.filter(|c| c.is_female());
              distribute_evenly(daughters)
          },
          DescentType::Bilateral => {
              // All children inherit
              distribute_evenly(deceased.children)
          }
      }
  }

  fn check_marriage_permissibility(person_a: &Person, person_b: &Person) -> Result<(), String> {
      let degree = calculate_kinship_degree(person_a, person_b);

      // Church forbids marriage to 6th cousins or closer
      if degree <= 6 {
          return Err("Too closely related by Church law".to_string());
      }

      // Check spiritual kinship
      if person_a.godparents.contains(&person_b.id) || person_b.godparents.contains(&person_a.id) {
          return Err("Spiritual kinship forbids marriage".to_string());
      }

      Ok(())
  }
  ```

**Educational Value:** Teaches how kinship organizes societies

**Fun Factor:** Complex family politics, inheritance scheming, alliance building

**Implementation:** Graph-based kinship network with descent rules

### Mechanic 2: Rites of Passage with Three Phases
**Academic Basis:** Van Gennep's rites of passage, Turner's liminality

**Translation:**
- **Major Life Transitions:**
  - Birth → Childhood
  - Childhood → Adulthood
  - Single → Married
  - Commoner → Knight/Guild Master
  - Living → Ancestor

- **Three-Phase Structure:**

  **1. Separation Phase:**
  - **Requirements:** Trigger event, preparation period
  - **Effects:**
    - NPC temporarily leaves normal social role
    - Reduced participation in regular activities
    - Special clothing/appearance
  - **Examples:**
    - Knighting: Vigil in chapel overnight (isolated from others)
    - Marriage: Bride leaves birth family home
    - Apprenticeship: Leave home, move to master's workshop

  **2. Liminal Phase (Threshold):**
  - **Characteristics:**
    - "Betwixt and between" status
    - Neither old nor new role
    - Heightened vulnerability and potential
    - Often involves tests, learning, transformation
  - **Mechanical Effects:**
    - NPC has temporary "Liminal" status
    - Can't perform old role OR new role fully
    - Special vulnerabilities (easier to influence, trauma possible)
    - Learning accelerated (open to change)
  - **Examples:**
    - Knighting: Dubbing ceremony, proving valor
    - Marriage: Wedding ceremony itself
    - Guild: Journeyman travels, creates masterwork

  **3. Reincorporation Phase:**
  - **Completion:** Public ceremony, community recognition
  - **Effects:**
    - New status officially granted
    - New rights, responsibilities, social position
    - Celebratory feast
    - New clothing/symbols of status
  - **Examples:**
    - Knighting: Feast, dubbed "Sir", can own land
    - Marriage: Consummation, couple recognized, can have children legitimately
    - Guild: Master status, can take apprentices, vote in guild

- **Implementation:**
  ```rust
  struct RiteOfPassage {
      rite_type: RiteType,
      subject: PersonId,
      phase: RitePhase,
      progress: f32,  // 0.0-1.0
      requirements_met: Vec<Requirement>,
  }

  enum RiteType {
      Birth,
      ComingOfAge,
      Marriage,
      Knighting,
      GuildMaster,
      Ordination,
      Death,
  }

  enum RitePhase {
      Separation { days_remaining: u32 },
      Liminal { transformation: Transformation },
      Reincorporation { feast_planned: bool },
  }

  struct Transformation {
      old_status: SocialStatus,
      new_status: SocialStatus,
      learning_multiplier: f32,  // Liminal = 2.0x
      vulnerability: f32,  // Liminal = 1.5x
  }

  fn advance_rite(rite: &mut RiteOfPassage, dt: f32) {
      match &mut rite.phase {
          RitePhase::Separation { days_remaining } => {
              *days_remaining -= 1;
              if *days_remaining == 0 {
                  rite.phase = RitePhase::Liminal {
                      transformation: create_transformation(rite.rite_type)
                  };
              }
          },
          RitePhase::Liminal { transformation } => {
              // Require specific actions/tests
              if rite.requirements_met.len() == rite.required_count() {
                  rite.phase = RitePhase::Reincorporation { feast_planned: false };
              }
          },
          RitePhase::Reincorporation { feast_planned } => {
              if *feast_planned {
                  // Complete the rite
                  apply_new_status(rite.subject, transformation.new_status);
                  trigger_feast_event();
              }
          }
      }
  }
  ```

**Educational Value:** Teaches universal pattern of life transitions

**Fun Factor:** Meaningful life milestones, transformation moments, social events

**Implementation:** Multi-phase state machine for life transitions

### Mechanic 3: Marriage Alliances and Negotiations
**Academic Basis:** Medieval marriage practices, kinship alliances

**Translation:**
- **Marriage as Alliance:**
  - Not just between individuals, but between families
  - Strategic tool for politics, economics, peace-making
  - Church requires consent, but families heavily influence

- **Marriage Negotiation Process:**

  **1. Proposal Phase:**
  - Family initiates based on:
    - Political alliance needs
    - Economic benefits (dowry, land, trade rights)
    - Status matching (marry within or slightly above class)
  - Check Church permissibility (kinship degree)

  **2. Negotiation Phase:**
  - Dowry amount (from bride's family)
  - Dower (husband's guarantee to wife)
  - Property settlements
  - Political terms (alliance duration, mutual defense)
  - **Failure conditions:** Terms unacceptable, family feud, bride/groom refuses

  **3. Betrothal:**
  - Formal promise before witnesses
  - Binding (breaking = scandal, social cost)
  - Waiting period (6 months typical)

  **4. Wedding Ceremony:**
  - Church ceremony (sacrament)
  - Public witnesses required
  - Vows exchanged

  **5. Consummation:**
  - Makes marriage fully valid
  - Feast celebration

- **Benefits of Marriage:**
  - Alliance between two kinship networks
  - Shared resources
  - Military cooperation
  - Trade privileges
  - Legitimacy for children
  - Inheritance rights

- **Implementation:**
  ```rust
  struct MarriageNegotiation {
      proposing_family: FamilyId,
      receiving_family: FamilyId,
      bride: PersonId,
      groom: PersonId,
      terms: MarriageTerms,
      status: NegotiationStatus,
  }

  struct MarriageTerms {
      dowry: u32,  // Gold from bride's family
      dower: u32,  // Security for wife if husband dies
      land_transfer: Option<LandId>,
      political_alliance: AllianceTerms,
  }

  enum NegotiationStatus {
      Proposed,
      Negotiating { rounds_remaining: u8 },
      Betrothed { wedding_date: Date },
      Rejected { reason: String },
  }

  fn evaluate_marriage_proposal(
      family: &Family,
      proposal: &MarriageNegotiation
  ) -> f32 {
      let mut score = 0.0;

      // Status matching
      let status_diff = (family.status as i32 - other_family.status as i32).abs();
      score += if status_diff <= 1 { 0.3 } else { -0.2 };

      // Economic benefit
      score += (proposal.terms.dowry as f32 / 1000.0) * 0.2;

      // Political benefit
      if family.enemies.intersects(&other_family.enemies) {
          score += 0.4;  // Common enemies = good alliance
      }

      // Personal consent (Church requires it)
      let bride_consent = ask_consent(proposal.bride);
      let groom_consent = ask_consent(proposal.groom);
      if !bride_consent || !groom_consent {
          score -= 0.5;  // Can override, but Church frowns
      }

      score
  }

  fn create_alliance(marriage: &Marriage) -> Alliance {
      Alliance {
          family_a: marriage.bride.family,
          family_b: marriage.groom.family,
          mutual_defense: true,
          trade_privileges: true,
          duration: AllianceDuration::WhileMarriageStands,
          strength: 0.7,  // Strong but not absolute
      }
  }
  ```

**Educational Value:** Shows how marriage organized medieval politics

**Fun Factor:** Dynastic scheming, arranged marriages, alliance gameplay

**Implementation:** Negotiation system with family interests and Church constraints

### Mechanic 4: Cultural Transmission and Skill Inheritance
**Academic Basis:** Cultural evolution theory, transmission mechanisms

**Translation:**
- **Three Transmission Pathways:**

  **Vertical (Parent → Child):**
  - **Speed:** Slow, conservative
  - **Reliability:** High (constant exposure)
  - **Examples:** Farming techniques, family trade secrets, religious beliefs
  - **Mechanic:** Children automatically learn parent skills at reduced rate

  ```
  child_skill_growth = parent_skill * 0.3 * time_spent_together
  # Farmer's child slowly learns farming
  ```

  **Horizontal (Peer → Peer):**
  - **Speed:** Fast, trendy
  - **Reliability:** Medium (depends on popularity)
  - **Examples:** Slang, fashion, music, fads
  - **Mechanic:** Skills/beliefs spread through social network by peer pressure

  ```
  adoption_chance = (friends_with_trait / total_friends) * trendiness
  # If most of your friends do something, you might too
  ```

  **Oblique (Teacher/Elder → Youth):**
  - **Speed:** Medium
  - **Reliability:** High (institutionalized)
  - **Examples:** Apprenticeships, Church teaching, schooling
  - **Mechanic:** Formal teaching with structured curriculum

  ```
  apprentice_skill_growth = master_skill * teaching_quality * 0.5 * time
  # Apprentice learns craft from master systematically
  ```

- **Multi-Source Learning:**
  - NPCs can learn from all three simultaneously
  - Conflicts possible (parent vs peer vs Church)
  - Cultural change emerges from transmission balance

- **Implementation:**
  ```rust
  struct SkillAcquisition {
      skill: SkillType,
      sources: Vec<LearningSource>,
      current_level: f32,
  }

  enum LearningSource {
      Vertical { parent: PersonId, exposure: f32 },
      Horizontal { peers: Vec<PersonId>, trend_strength: f32 },
      Oblique { teacher: PersonId, curriculum: Curriculum },
  }

  fn update_skill_learning(person: &mut Person, dt: f32) {
      for skill_acq in &mut person.learning {
          let mut growth = 0.0;

          for source in &skill_acq.sources {
              match source {
                  LearningSource::Vertical { parent, exposure } => {
                      let parent_level = get_skill(*parent, skill_acq.skill);
                      growth += parent_level * 0.3 * exposure * dt;
                  },
                  LearningSource::Horizontal { peers, trend_strength } => {
                      let peer_avg = peers.iter()
                          .map(|p| get_skill(*p, skill_acq.skill))
                          .sum::<f32>() / peers.len() as f32;
                      growth += peer_avg * trend_strength * 0.5 * dt;
                  },
                  LearningSource::Oblique { teacher, curriculum } => {
                      let teacher_level = get_skill(*teacher, skill_acq.skill);
                      let teaching_quality = curriculum.effectiveness;
                      growth += teacher_level * teaching_quality * 0.5 * dt;
                  }
              }
          }

          skill_acq.current_level = (skill_acq.current_level + growth).min(100.0);
      }
  }
  ```

**Educational Value:** Shows how culture is transmitted and changes

**Fun Factor:** Apprenticeship systems, family legacy, cultural shifts

**Implementation:** Multi-pathway skill acquisition with different rates

### Mechanic 5: Ritual Calendar and Social Cohesion
**Academic Basis:** Ritual studies, social functions of ceremony

**Translation:**
- **Annual Ritual Calendar:**
  - Regular events throughout year
  - Community participation
  - Social cohesion effects

- **Ritual Types:**

  **Religious Festivals:**
  - Christmas, Easter, Saints' Days
  - Mass attendance
  - **Effect:** +10 faith, +5 community cohesion
  - **Requirement:** Church building, priest

  **Harvest Festivals:**
  - Lammas (Aug 1 - first bread from harvest)
  - Michaelmas (Sep 29 - end of harvest)
  - **Effect:** +15 community cohesion, +10 morale
  - **Requirement:** Successful harvest

  **Life Cycle Rituals:**
  - Baptisms, weddings, funerals
  - **Effect:** Variable based on importance
  - **Requirement:** Priest, family participation

  **Guild Ceremonies:**
  - Apprentice promotions
  - Masterwork presentations
  - **Effect:** +10 guild loyalty, status change
  - **Requirement:** Guild hall

- **Participation Mechanics:**
  - Higher participation = stronger effects
  - Skipping rituals = social cost (gossip, reputation)
  - Community pressure to attend

- **Implementation:**
  ```rust
  struct RitualCalendar {
      annual_events: Vec<ScheduledRitual>,
      spontaneous_events: Vec<RitualEvent>,
  }

  struct ScheduledRitual {
      name: String,
      date: (Month, Day),
      ritual_type: RitualType,
      required_participation: f32,  // 0.0-1.0
      effects: Vec<SocialEffect>,
  }

  enum RitualType {
      Religious,
      Harvest,
      LifeCycle,
      Guild,
      Feudal,
  }

  struct SocialEffect {
      target: EffectTarget,
      stat: SocialStat,
      change: i32,
  }

  enum SocialStat {
      CommunityChosen,
      Faith,
      Morale,
      GuildLoyalty,
  }

  fn execute_ritual(ritual: &ScheduledRitual, participants: &[PersonId]) {
      let participation_rate = participants.len() as f32 / village_population() as f32;

      if participation_rate < ritual.required_participation {
          // Ritual fails, social shame
          for person in participants {
              modify_reputation(person, -5);
          }
          return;
      }

      // Apply effects scaled by participation
      for effect in &ritual.effects {
          let scaled_change = (effect.change as f32 * participation_rate) as i32;

          match effect.target {
              EffectTarget::AllParticipants => {
                  for person in participants {
                      modify_stat(person, effect.stat, scaled_change);
                  }
              },
              EffectTarget::EntireVillage => {
                  modify_village_stat(effect.stat, scaled_change);
              }
          }
      }

      // Collective effervescence: emotional intensity from group participation
      if participation_rate > 0.8 {
          trigger_collective_effervescence(participants);  // Bonus morale, memorable
      }
  }

  fn calculate_social_pressure_to_attend(person: &Person, ritual: &RitualEvent) -> f32 {
      let mut pressure = ritual.base_importance;

      // Family members attending?
      let family_attending = person.family.filter(|f| f.attending_ritual).count();
      pressure += family_attending as f32 * 0.1;

      // Community expectation
      if ritual.expected_attendance > 0.9 {
          pressure += 0.3;  // "Everyone" goes
      }

      // Personal status (high status = more expected)
      pressure += person.status as f32 * 0.05;

      pressure
  }
  ```

**Educational Value:** Shows role of ritual in maintaining society

**Fun Factor:** Calendar events, community pressure, social consequences

**Implementation:** Event calendar with participation tracking and social effects

### Pattern: Rites of Passage → State Machine
**Approach:**
1. Map three phases to game states
2. Separation = status removal
3. Liminality = transformation state with special rules
4. Reincorporation = new status granted
5. Community witnesses required

**Example:**
```
# Anthropology: Knighting ritual
# Game: Vigil (separation) → Dubbing (liminal test) → Feast (new status)
# Mechanic: Multi-day event with requirements and social effects
```

---

### Pattern: Cultural Transmission → Learning System
**Approach:**
1. Identify three pathways (vertical, horizontal, oblique)
2. Each has different speed, reliability, content
3. Skills/beliefs learned through multiple simultaneous sources
4. Cultural change emerges from transmission dynamics

**Example:**
```
# Anthropology: Apprenticeship = oblique transmission
# Game: Master teaches apprentice systematically, faster than parent teaching
# Result: Specialized skills spread through guild system
```
