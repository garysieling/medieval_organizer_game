# Lit Review: Historical Campaigns of Community Organizing — Figures, Wins, Losses, and What Worked

**Survey Date:** 2026-10-31
**Agent:** Literature Survey Agent
**Domain:** Social Movement History / Community Organizing Case Studies
**Backlog items:** Community organizing, power recognizes power, organizing vs mobilizing, injustice & labor organizing
**Companion doc:** [organizing-countertactics.md](organizing-countertactics.md) — the repression/infiltration side
**Confidence:** High on well-documented movements; Medium on fine-grained tactical details

## Purpose

A wide-net survey of real organizing campaigns: who organized, what they ran, what they won, what they lost, and — most importantly — the recurring strategic patterns that separated wins from losses. Prior reviews (sprint-3, sprint-4, sprint-7) covered organizing *theory* (Alinsky, Ganz, McAlevey, Han). This one covers the **historical record**.

## Part 1: Figures and Movements (the wide net)

### Anti-Apartheid (South Africa, 1948–1994)
**Figures:** Nelson Mandela, Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Albert Luthuli, Steve Biko (Black Consciousness), Desmond Tutu (church-based resistance), Helen Joseph, Winnie Mandela, the UDF leadership, and thousands of unnamed shop stewards in COSATU unions.
**Key orgs:** ANC, ANC Youth League, SAIC, Congress Alliance, UDF (United Democratic Front, 1983), COSATU.
**Campaigns:** Defiance Campaign (1952), Congress of the People / Freedom Charter (1955), Sharpeville aftermath, Soweto uprising (1976), UDF township organizing (1983–89), mass stayaways and strikes, international divestment/sanctions campaign.
**Wins:** Ended apartheid; 1994 universal elections; transferred power without full civil war.
**Losses:** Armed struggle (MK) achieved little militarily and cost the movement decades of imprisoned leadership; Sharpeville (1960) drove movement into exile — an entire generation organized from outside; movement could not deliver economic equality post-1994.

### US Civil Rights Movement (1954–1968)
**Figures:** Rosa Parks (organizer, not accidental actor — trained at Highlander Folk School), Martin Luther King Jr., Ella Baker, Fred Shuttlesworth, Wyatt Tee Walker, James Bevel, Diane Nash, John Lewis, Bob Moses, Fannie Lou Hamer, Bayard Rustin.
**Key orgs:** NAACP (legal/institutional), SCLC (church-based direct action), SNCC (grassroots/student), CORE, MIA, Highlander Folk School (training infrastructure).
**Campaigns:** Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56), sit-in wave (1960), Freedom Rides (1961), Albany (1961–62 — the failure), Birmingham/Project C (1963), March on Washington (1963), Freedom Summer (1964), Selma (1965), Poor People's Campaign (1968 — unfinished).
**Wins:** Civil Rights Act 1964, Voting Rights Act 1965, dismantled legal segregation.
**Losses:** Albany campaign (diffuse goals, savvy opponent Laurie Pritchett who avoided photogenic violence); Chicago open-housing campaign 1966 (met organized white resistance, limited wins); economic demands never won; movement's grassroots depth eroded after 1965 as attention moved to media/politics.

### Farmworkers Movement (1962–1980s)
**Figures:** Cesar Chavez, Dolores Huerta (lead negotiator and organizer — "Sí se puede"), Larry Itliong (Filipino organizer who started the Delano strike), Philip Vera Cruz, Gil Padilla.
**Key orgs:** NFWA → UFWOC → UFW; AWOC (Filipino); community support committees in 30+ cities.
**Campaigns:** Delano grape strike (1965–70), international grape boycott (the most effective consumer boycott in US labor history), 280-mile Sacramento march (1966), Chavez's fasts (1968, 1972), lettuce boycott (1970s), Prop 14 campaign (1976 — lost).
**Wins:** First farmworker union contracts in California history (1970 table-grape contracts, ~150 growers); California Agricultural Labor Relations Act 1975 (first law giving farmworkers collective bargaining rights); raised farmworker conditions to national issue.
**Losses:** Teamster collusion agreements with growers (jurisdictional raiding, violence at pickets); UFW membership collapsed in the 1980s (from ~100k to under 10k); Chavez's late-career authoritarianism, purges, and "the Game" (Synanon-inspired control) gutted the leadership pipeline; most gains eroded within a decade.
**Lesson cluster:** Boycott worked because it bypassed the workplace (where strikers were replaceable) and hit the brand (Schenley/DiGiorgio sold liquor nationally — consumer-facing, vulnerable). Wins were institutional (contracts, law) and thus decapitable when enforcement weakened.

### Stonewall & Gay Liberation (1969–)
**Figures:** Marsha P. Johnson, Sylvia Rivera (STAR), Stormé DeLarverie, Bob Kohler, Frank Kameny & Barbara Gittings (pre-Stonewall homophile movement — the "annual reminder" pickets), the Mattachine Society and Daughters of Bilitis networks that preceded the riot.
**Key orgs:** Mattachine Society, Daughters of Bilitis, GLF (1969), GAA (1969, split for being more focused), STAR (1970), later Lambda Legal, ACT UP (1987).
**Campaigns:** Stonewall uprising itself (spontaneous riot, not organized — but *converted* into organized power within weeks), Christopher Street Liberation Day (1970 → Pride), GAA "zaps" (targeted disruptions), APA depathologizing campaign (won 1973), ACT UP die-ins (won FDA drug trial reform).
**Wins:** Catalytic event → national movement within months; depathologization 1973; eventually marriage equality 2015 (decades-long institutional campaign by successors).
**Losses:** GLF collapsed within ~3 years — non-hierarchical consensus meetings became paralyzed, splintered into cells; trans people and people of color were pushed out of the "respectable" gay rights orgs they helped spark (STAR's concerns — housing, survival — sidelined for decades); AIDS crisis nearly destroyed the movement's institutional base in the 80s.
**Lesson cluster:** A riot creates *attention*, orgs create *durable power*. GLF (radical, leaderless, multi-issue) lost people to GAA (single-issue, structured) — echoing Han's organizing-vs-mobilizing split decades before it was theorized.

### Women's Rights — First Wave (property, suffrage, 1839–1920)
**Figures:** Betsy Love Allen (Chickasaw precedent case behind the first MWPA), Piety Hadley (pushed the 1839 Mississippi act), Ernestine Rose, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony, Lucretia Mott, Sojourner Truth, Alice Paul, Lucy Burns, Ida B. Wells (anti-lynching + suffrage).
**Campaigns:** Married Women's Property Acts state-by-state (Mississippi 1839 first — but driven by *debt shielding*, not feminism; NY 1848 became the template); Seneca Falls 1848; suffrage split (NAWSA state-by-state lobbying vs NWP militant tactics — picketing the White House, arrests, hunger strikes, force-feeding → "Night of Terror" 1917); 19th Amendment 1920.
**Wins:** MWPA across nearly all states 1850–1920; 19th Amendment.
**Losses:** ERA never ratified (1923 drafted, failed by 1982 deadline); suffrage victory was racially hollow — Black women remained disenfranchised in practice until 1965 VRA; the post-1920 movement collapsed into fragmentation (protective labor law vs equal rights split).
**Lesson cluster:** **The first MWPA won on elite self-interest, not moral argument** (protecting property from creditors). Early legal wins often ride on interests of the powerful — the movement's job is to make that happen and then broaden the frame.

### Women in Ministry (preaching rights, 1650s–present)
**Figures:** Anne Hutchinson (religious dissent, 1630s), Quaker women preachers (Margaret Fell — women preached in Quaker tradition from 1650s), Jarena Lee (first Black woman preacher authorized by AME, 1819), Antoinette Brown Blackwell (first ordained woman in a major US denomination, 1853 — though unrecognized denominationally), Olympia Brown (first denominational ordination, Universalist 1863), Amanda Berry Smith (Holiness evangelist), Aimee Semple McPherson (founded Foursquare, 1920s), the "Philadelphia Eleven" (irregular Episcopal ordinations 1974), Pauli Murray (first Black woman Episcopal priest, 1977).
**Pattern:** Women preached first in *marginal/new* movements (Quakers, Free Will Baptists, Holiness, Pentecostal, new denominations) where revival urgency trumped gatekeeping — then institutional denominations absorbed the practice slowly (Methodists full clergy 1956, Presbyterian 1956, Episcopal 1977, ELCA 1970, RCC/Orthodox/Southern Baptist never).
**Wins:** Ordination across most mainline Protestant denominations by 1980.
**Losses:** Mainline gatekeeping took 100+ years; "firsts" were often tokenized and isolated (Brown Blackwell resigned within a year); several denominations still bar women — the fight continues inside institutions rather than through them.
**Lesson cluster:** **New institutions move faster than old ones reform.** Frontier/revival religions granted women pulpits because they needed labor and lacked gatekeeping machinery; establishment churches required generational fights. Organizing lesson: sometimes you build the new vessel rather than reform the old.

### Industrial Areas Foundation / Broad-Based Organizing (1940–present)
**Figures:** Saul Alinsky (Back of the Yards Council 1939, IAF 1940, *Rules for Radicals* 1971), Ed Chambers (professionalized training), Ernesto Cortes Jr. (COPS San Antonio 1974 — the modern faith-based model), Sr. Christine Stephens, Mike Gecan (East Brooklyn Congregations), Arnie Graf (BUILD Baltimore).
**Campaigns:** Back of the Yards (packinghouse neighborhood, union+church alliance); COPS vs San Antonio's Good Government League — won drainage infrastructure for flooded Westside barrios and broke Anglo elite monopoly (won via *accountability sessions* and bloc voting, not protest); East Brooklyn Congregations' Nehemiah Homes (5,000+ affordable homes — the largest low-income housing build in US history); Capital IDEA job training (Austin Interfaith); living wage ordinances nationwide via IAF/PICO networks.
**Wins:** Durable — COPS/Metro still powerful 50 years later; produced a replicable model (relational meetings, house meetings, accountability sessions) used worldwide.
**Losses/limits:** Deliberately non-ideological → wins are local and pragmatic, never systemic; Alinsky's own model relied on *imported* professional organizers (I.T.O. — organizer as external entrepreneur) which Cortes partially fixed via congregation bases; famously hostile to movement-style moral crusading, so misses wave moments.
**Lesson cluster:** **Institutional anchoring = durability.** Tying orgs to congregations (which don't move and already have trust, money, buildings) solved the #1 organizing failure mode — attrition. COPS' power came from being a permanent actor that politicians *had* to reckon with across election cycles.

### Labor Movement (US, 1880s–1950s for peak wins)
**Figures:** Mother Jones, Eugene Debs, Big Bill Haywood (IWW), Lucy Parsons, John L. Lewis, Sidney Hillman, the Reuther brothers, Harry Bridges (ILWU), A. Philip Randolph (Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters — 12-year fight, won 1937; then threatened March on Washington 1941 to win FEPC), Frances Perkins (labor wins written into New Deal).
**Campaigns:** Haymarket 1886, Homestead 1892 (lost — Pinkerton/private armies), Pullman 1894 (lost — federal injunction + troops), Uprising of the 20,000 (NY garment workers, mostly immigrant women, 1909 — won), Flint sit-down strike 1936–37 (won — the breakthrough: *occupying* instead of picketing), CIO mass organizing 1935–40, Wagner Act 1935, GM/UAW contracts.
**Wins:** 8-hour day, weekend, Wagner Act, mass industrial unionism, mid-century labor power.
**Losses:** Company unions + Mohawk Valley Formula (see countertactics doc); Wagner Act excluded agricultural + domestic workers (racial carve-out); Taft-Hartley 1947 crippled organizing (right-to-work laws); labor never won universal wins in the South.
**Lesson cluster:** Flint sit-down = **tactical innovation defeating power asymmetry** — occupying the plant meant police couldn't attack without destroying the employer's capital. Tactics that make repression costly for the target work.

### Abolition (1830s–1865)
**Figures:** Frederick Douglass, William Lloyd Garrison, Harriet Tubman, John Brown (the violent edge), Sojourner Truth, the Grimké sisters (first women to speak publicly to "promiscuous" audiences — linking abolition + women's rights), plus Black-led mutual aid and Underground Railroad networks.
**Campaigns:** Garrison's *Liberator* + moral suasion era; petition campaigns to Congress (Adams's gag-rule fights — thousands of petitions); Underground Railroad (direct action infrastructure); political abolitionism (Liberty → Free Soil → Republican parties); fugitive slave rescues.
**Wins:** Emancipation — though through war, which complicates "movement wins" claims.
**Losses:** Moral suasion alone failed for 30 years (Garrison himself concluded the system couldn't be shamed); immediate post-war Reconstruction gains were violently reversed when organizing pressure relaxed.
**Lesson cluster:** Multi-pronged movements win: moral framing + political party + direct action + law. Abolition is the template of "outside agitation makes inside reform possible."

### Climate Organizing (2006–present)
**Figures:** Bill McKibben (350.org), Greta Thunberg (Fridays for Future), Varshini Prakash (Sunrise Movement), the Standing Rock water protectors (Indigenous-led — LaDonna Brave Bull Allard, Dallas Goldtooth), XR founders (Roger Hallam, Gail Bradbrook).
**Campaigns:** Keystone XL pipeline (2011–2021 — delayed then cancelled; a rare clear win — sat-in waves + rancher alliances + litigation), fossil fuel divestment ($40T+ committed assets — narrative win, contested material effect), Standing Rock (2016–17 — pipeline completed after change in administration, but catalyzed global Indigenous solidarity network), Sunrise sit-ins → Green New Deal agenda-setting (2018–19 — policy not passed, frame changed), XR London 2019 (public concern spike per UK polling), anti-coal campaigns (Beyond Coal — biggest concrete win: blocked/retired 65%+ of US coal plants).
**Wins:** Agenda-setting (climate now top-tier issue); pipeline delays/cancellations; coal plant retirements; GND frame.
**Losses:** No binding US federal climate legislation until IRA 2021 (and it passed via legislative deal-making, not street pressure); divestment's material effect on fossil industry is contested (systematic review: real effects are social-license erosion and agenda-setting, not capital starvation); XR peaked and fragmented on strategy disputes.
**Lesson cluster:** **Disruption gets attention; attention ≠ policy.** The wins (Keystone, coal) had *specific blockable targets* + coalitions with affected landowners/Indigenous rights-holders. The losses (national legislation, global treaties) were diffuse asks against diffuse power — the Albany pattern.

### Bonus quick-list (worth a deep-dive later)
- **Gandhi's Indian campaigns** — Salt March 1930 (the perfect engineered campaign: symbolic, participatory, economically real, irresistible press narrative); also failures (1922 Chauri Chaura violence → he called off the whole movement to preserve nonviolent discipline).
- **Otpor Serbia 2000** — humor ("resistance marketing"), branding, decentralized cells, police/military defection outreach → Milošević fell. Exported via CANVAS trainings to Georgia/Ukraine/Egypt (mixed results).
- **Anti-colonial** — see sprint-4 review; add Algerian FLN (won militarily/diplomatically, at catastrophic cost), Indian independence (nonviolent + mutiny + British exhaustion).
- **Suffrage militants (UK)** — Pankhursts' WSPU: window-breaking, arson, hunger strikes; won votes in 1918/1928 but causality is contested (wartime contribution vs militancy).
- **ACT UP** — the single most effective disease-policy movement ever: die-ins, "SILENCE=DEATH," infiltrating FDA/NIH processes; won trial-design reform, accelerated approvals.
- **Disability rights** — 1977 Section 504 sit-in (25-day occupation of HEW offices — longest occupation of a federal building; won enforcement regs); Capitol Crawl 1990 → ADA. Coalition with Black Panthers feeding protesters is a famous cross-movement solidarity node.
- **Tenant organizing** — NYC rent strikes (1904–1920s → first rent control); St. Louis 1969 rent strike; current KC Tenants.

## Part 2: What Worked / What Didn't (cross-case synthesis)

### High-level strategy patterns

**WHAT WORKED**

1. **Winning on the enemy's terrain of weakness, not yours of strength.**
   Farmworkers couldn't win picket-line fights (replaceable workforce) — won via consumer boycott hitting national brands. Birmingham couldn't beat Bull Connor politically — forced him to *overreact* visibly. UFW and SCLC both converted opponent's strength (violence, arrests) into their own media asset. (*Political jiu-jitsu* — Sharp's term.)

2. **Specific, winnable, escalating demands.**
   Albany failed (desegregate *everything*); Birmingham won (desegregate downtown stores — concrete, measurable, deadline-able). COPS won drainage ditches, not "justice." Winnable asks → demonstrated wins → recruitment flywheel. McAlevey: structure tests prove power incrementally.

3. **Institutional anchoring / durable infrastructure.**
   The longest-lived orgs were embedded in institutions that don't leave: COPS in parishes, SCLC in Black churches, Highlander as training base, unions in workplaces. GLF (no institutional base) died in 3 years; COPS is 50+.

4. **Coalitions across the "natural" base.**
   Keystone killed by rancher+Indigenous+environmentalist alliance. UDF united black/colored/Indian + church + union + student. Filipino AWOC + Latino NFWA merger made the grape strike work. Coalition breadth predicted resilience against decapitation.

5. **Elite defection / leverage over the power's dependencies.**
   Chenoweth's core finding: nonviolent campaigns win by **pulling away the pillars** — civil servants, security forces, business, churches, international allies. Apartheid fell when the *economy* (sanctions + strikes + capital flight) made the status quo costlier than reform. The 3.5% figure is a proxy: what matters is that participation is broad *enough* to reach into the regime's own base.

6. **Nonviolent discipline as strategic asset.**
   NAVCO data: ~53% success for nonviolent vs ~26% for violent campaigns (1900–2006). Nonviolence lowers participation barriers (everyone can join), raises regime repression costs (backfire), and enables defection. BUT it's not magic: Bahrain mobilized 6%+ and lost. Discipline matters most when the opponent *wants* you violent (see countertactics).

7. **Parallel institution building, not just protest.**
   Abolition built political parties + railroads; farmworkers built clinics/service centers; STAR housed street youth; unions built mutual aid before strikes. Orgs that *meet needs* retain members through losses.

**WHAT FAILED / RECURRING LOSSES**

1. **Diffuse asks / no clear target** — Albany, national climate legislation, GLF's everything-liberation. Power can't be pressured if it can't say yes to anything specific.

2. **Spontaneity without organization** — Stonewall the riot was unplanned; the movement won because orgs formed *after*. Riots create attention spikes, not power (Rodney King 1992, Ferguson uprisings: attention without durable org → limited structural yield).

3. **Charismatic leader dependency** — Chavez's late UFW (purges, no succession); post-King SCLC drift; movements decapitated by assassination *when* they lacked distributed leadership (vs SNCC's "group-centered leadership" — Ella Baker's explicit counter-model).

4. **Victory-then-disband** — suffrage movement after 1920; post-apartheid demobilization; divestment orgs after commitments. Gains evaporate without enforcement infrastructure (Reconstruction's fate; farm contracts eroding in the 80s).

5. **Failing to counter internal sabotage** — see countertactics doc: COINTELPRO's biggest wins weren't raids but *trust destruction* — movements tore themselves apart suspecting infiltrators. Groups with no counter-infiltration doctrine paid the highest price.

6. **Purity/expulsion spirals** — GLF factionalism; suffrage split's costs (though split also created complementary wings — splits are double-edged); XR's strategic schisms. Movements die more from self-inflicted fission than from external defeat.

7. **Tactical stagnation** — opponent adapts. Marches lost marginal power each repetition post-1963; boycott only worked while it was novel; XR's disruption fatigue. Winning movements rotate the repertoire (Sharp's 198 methods exist for this reason).

### The deep regularities (for game abstraction)

- **Power = organized relationships, not crowds.** Every durable win traces to *structure* (committees, locals, chapters, unions), not turnout. GLF's 50-person consensus meetings built nothing; GAA's committee system won concrete items.
- **Perceived power > actual power at negotiation time** (Alinsky Rule 1 confirmed historically): Bull Connor, Schenley, city councils all moved when *perceived* threat crossed a threshold — which is why structure tests and media visibility matter so much.
- **Repression is a wildcard** — it can destroy a movement (Sharpeville exile generation) *or* backfire into recruitment (Birmingham children's crusade, Night of Terror). The difference: whether the movement *planned* for repression and made it costly (photo-able, unjust, against sympathetic victims).
- **Campaigns are the unit, not movements.** Movements are brands/umbrellas; power is built campaign-by-campaign with structure tests → escalation → demand → settlement → next.
- **Losses teach, if org survives** — Albany's failure directly produced Birmingham's design; AWOC/NFWA strike failures produced boycott innovation. Organizational *learning capacity* may be the truest win predictor.

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Campaign structure
Model organizing as discrete **campaigns** with: a concrete target org, a demand (win/losable), escalation ladder (petition → structure test → strike/boycott → confrontation), and a settlement step. Success yields `organized_power` + recruitment; failure yields learning (unlocks better tactics — Albany→Birmingham) but costs morale.

### Mechanic 2: Pillars of support
Power-holders depend on named pillars (economy, military/guard, clergy, media, bureaucracy). Campaigns win not by defeating the leader but by **pulling pillars** — each pillar has a loyalty score shifted by actions (strikes hit economy; public suffering hits legitimacy; defection outreach hits security). Medieval: lord's pillars = retainers, church, guilds, neighboring lords.

### Mechanic 3: Political jiu-jitsu / repression backfire
When authority represses a disciplined nonviolent action, roll on visibility × sympathy: high-visibility repression of sympathetic actors converts fence-sitters, raises movement reputation, and can trigger pillar defection. Undisciplined/violent actions flip it — repression gains legitimacy. This makes *discipline* a player-managed stat, and provocateurs (countertactics doc) a real threat.

### Mechanic 4: Institutional anchoring
Orgs anchored to an institution (church, guild, mutual aid society) decay slowly and survive leader loss; unanchored orgs decay fast post-win. Player tradeoff: anchoring costs resources/time up front, pays off in durability. GLF-vs-COPS as the extreme cases.

### Mechanic 5: Leadership pipeline vs charismatic bottleneck
Leaders have capacity caps. Developing leaders costs actions but multiplies org capacity and provides decapitation insurance. Charismatic-leader mode is faster early but fragile (Chavez problem: late-game purge events).

### Mechanic 6: Ask specificity
Demands range diffuse→specific. Specific demands have lower ceiling but higher success chance and grant structure-test wins; diffuse demands often fail but shift the Overton frame (loss that still moves public opinion). Lets players run "winnable" vs "transformational" campaigns with different payoffs.

### Mechanic 7: Coalition breadth
Alliances with out-group orgs multiply resilience (harder to decapitate, wider participation pool) but add coordination friction (slower decisions, ideological drift events). Rancher+Indigenous alliance as the exemplar.
