## Sources

### 1. Hindu-Arabic Numerals Reach Europe (Cambridge WP 2019; AHJ; BSHM 2025)
- Developed in India (~5th c.), passed through the Islamic world; sporadic European use 10th-12th c.
- **Leonardo of Pisa's *Liber Abaci* (1202)** taught them for commerce, though it wasn't widely read at first
- Spread through **abacus schools** (*scuole d'abaco*) training merchants' sons in Italian cities; adoption was **gradual over ~300 years**
- **Resistance:** the **1299 Florence moneychangers' guild statute** barred the new numerals in account books, seen as **easier to forge** (a 0 becomes a 6 or 9); Roman numerals in words were harder to alter
- Some religious/cultural suspicion of "infidel" numerals
- **Relevance:** CRITICAL - real historical conflict between number systems: **trust and fraud**, not just efficiency

### 2. Counting Boards and the Exchequer (*Dialogus de Scaccario*; Accounting History Review; LSE CDAM)
- England's Exchequer was named for the **chequered cloth** on its table, a giant counting board on which sheriffs' accounts were **calculated with counters** twice a year
- Roman numerals were for **recording**; calculation happened **on the board** (abacus/jetons), so Roman numerals weren't as clumsy as they look
- Charge-and-discharge accounting (Exchequer) vs later double-entry bookkeeping (Italian, printed by Pacioli 1494)
- **Relevance:** HIGH - number systems come paired with **tools and institutions**; switching means retraining clerks

### 3. Tally Sticks (Science Museum; *Dialogus*)
- Hazel sticks **notched by amount** (a palm-width notch = £1,000, a thumb-width = £100, ... ), then **split lengthwise**: the payer kept the *foil*, the Exchequer kept the *stock*
- The halves only match each other, a **forgery-proof receipt** usable by the illiterate; used from the 12th to the 19th c.
- **Relevance:** CRITICAL - a third "number system" for non-literate groups, with built-in trust mechanics

### 4. Dragomans and Interpreters (*J. Medieval Worlds* 2020; Salicrú; Wikipedia)
- **Dragomans**: interpreters/guides between Arabic-, Turkish-, Persian-speaking polities and European merchants and embassies
- Their value was **trust**, not just language: they vouched, mediated disputes, knew customs; often from **in-between communities** (Ottoman Greeks; converts; Jews and Mudéjars in Iberia)
- Distrusted by both sides as potential double agents; families held the role across generations
- **Relevance:** CRITICAL - translator is a relationship broker whose power comes from sitting between groups

## Mechanics

### Number systems as culture traits
| System | Strength | Weakness | Users |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tally notches | illiterate-friendly, forgery-proof receipts | can't calculate | peasants, exchequers |
| Roman numerals + counting board | institutional standard, hard to alter in words | needs a board to calculate | church, crown clerks |
| Hindu-Arabic (positional) | fast written calculation, enables bookkeeping/credit | easy to forge, "foreign" | merchants, abacus schools |

- Each faction/org has a **numeracy system** and a **literacy level**
- **Cross-system trade** has friction: error and fraud chance rises unless a translator/clerk who knows both is present
- **Adoption** spreads through schools and merchant success; guilds may **ban** it (Florence 1299), which gives conflict and Galileo-style adoption politics

### Translator role
- Translators have skill per language/number system and **trust** scores with each side
- Deals through a trusted translator have lower friction and fraud; a translator can also **skew** a deal (betrayal risk)
- Translators accumulate relationships across groups, so they're natural diplomats, spies, or organizers who bridge communities

### Language learning (slow word introduction)
- Mirrors the "l'avion my uncle flew" backlog idea: dialogue mixes in foreign words gradually as a character's familiarity grows; the player learns real words in context

## Design Proposals

1. **Numeracy trait per faction** (tally / Roman+board / positional) affecting trade friction, accounting accuracy, and fraud
2. **Abacus school** building that spreads positional numerals and creates merchant-clerk vocations
3. **Guild ban event** (Florence 1299 style): incumbents resist a better system for real reasons (forgery), which lets the "better" tech lose for a while
4. **Tally-stick receipts** as items: split, matched later to settle disputes; works for illiterate characters
5. **Translator/dragoman vocation** with dual trust scores; an in-between community supplies most translators
6. **Gradual vocabulary** in dialogue for foreign groups, as an educational language-learning layer

## Rating
**High** - number systems become tradeoffs with real historical reasons (trust, fraud, tools, institutions), and the translator role turns cultural difference into relationship gameplay.
