## Purpose

The sprint-4 review covered anti-colonial *theory* (Fanon, Freire, Scott, Said). This covers what anti-colonial movements **actually did** — the networks, campaigns, wins, and losses — and the counter-insurgency doctrines developed against them, which are the ancestor of the modern counter-playbook.

## Case 1: Haiti (1791–1804) — the only successful slave revolution

**The organizing layer beneath the famous night:**
- The uprising was not spontaneous. Commandeurs, coachmen, and servants from Plaine du Nord plantations had been **meeting regularly** — a pre-existing network of the enslaved elite, coordinating with maroon communities in the hills. Plantation work gangs were the org infrastructure.
- **Bois Caïman (Aug 21, 1791)** fused organization with religion: Dutty Boukman's liberty sermon, mambo Cécile Fatiman's blood oath (liberty or death, secrecy), pig sacrifice. The ceremony did three jobs at once: commitment device (oath binding), leadership investiture, and cross-ethnic unification (Vodou was the *only* structure connecting enslaved Africans across ethnic lines — Ezili Kawoulo is specifically the patron of secret societies).
- **Movement disinfo**: the conspirators printed a fake news sheet claiming the king had granted slaves 3 free days/week and abolished whipping — manufacturing a grievance-boosting lie to provoke revolt when authorities inevitably denied it. A false-flag in reverse: invented *concession* to fuel anger at its "revocation."
- **Escalation**: within a week, ~15,000 insurgents burned 184 plantations. Boukman was killed within months; the revolution's later success ran through Toussaint, Dessalines — and 13 years of war.
- **Win**: the only successful slave revolution in history; abolished slavery *and* colonialism.
- **Cost/loss**: France extorted an indemnity (1825, ~$21B modern equivalent) that crippled Haiti for a century — the enforcement problem in its most brutal form: the win was counter-attacked through *debt*. Foreign recognition withheld (US didn't recognize until 1862).
- **Lesson:** sacred infrastructure + secret networks + external-war timing; but post-victory isolation can be weaponized — recognition, trade, and debt are pillars too.

## Case 2: Ireland's Land War (1879–82) — where "boycott" was invented

- **The mechanism**: the Land League (Davitt, Parnell) ran rent strikes + **social ostracism** — Parnell prescribed it in the Ennis speech (Sept 1880): "putting him into a moral Coventry." Captain Boycott was targeted days later; his name became the tactic.
- **Key detail — boycotting was mostly insider discipline**: it was used more often against *tenant farmers who took evicted farms* than against landlords. The tactic's primary function was enforcing in-group solidarity — the boycott's edge points inward. (This matches the charivari function in sprint-7: community sanctions police the community's own defectors.)
- **Organizational redundancy — the Ladies' Land League**: Anna Parnell founded the Ladies' Land League explicitly to run the movement *when the men's leaders were jailed* — which they were (1881, League banned). The women's org ran operations, and was arguably more militant than the men's. Parnell later shut it down — cutting the movement's own redundancy to preserve control (the charismatic-bottleneck problem striking its own insurance policy).
- **Wins**: land acts of 1881, then the Land Purchase acts — tenant ownership transferred over decades; arguably the most successful anti-landlord campaign in Western history, achieved mostly without armed force.
- **Lesson:** nonviolent ostracism works *where the community is dense enough to make isolation hurt* — it's a networked-community tactic, not an individual one. And design succession infrastructure *before* the leaders are jailed.

## Case 3: Algeria / FLN (1954–62) — the cell structure and its counter-doctrine

- **Structure**: classic clandestine cell system — the FLN's politico-administrative organization built a parallel state (taxes, justice, education) underneath French administration; militants organized in compartmentalized cells where members knew only their own level.
- **The Soummam conference (Aug 1956)**: strategic pivot to urban terrorism — bring the war to Algiers where the cameras were.
- **The two Battles of Algiers (1957)** — counter-insurgency in two registers:
  - **Jan–Mar 1957**: Massu's 10th Paras, extraordinary police powers, mass arrests and torture. Broke the bombing wave — at a price.
  - **Apr–Oct 1957**: Godard's surgical phase — infiltration ("bleuite": turned FLN agents run back into the network as double agents), disinformation, precise cell mapping. Dismantled the network by October.
- **The strategic defeat inside the tactical win**: France won Algiers and lost Algeria. Torture corroded French legitimacy at home (the Sartre/press scandals), radicalized the population being "pacified," and the FLN's diplomatic/political wing won where its cells lost. Independence 1962.
- **The Galula paradox**: David Galula's hearts-and-minds doctrine (drawn from his Kabylie sector) became the canonical counter-insurgency manual — *Counterinsurgency Warfare* (1964) literally frames counter-organizing as competitive organization: the counter-insurgent must out-organize the insurgency for the population. Yet scholarship shows even Galula's showcase was far less effective than the myth — the two registers (brutal vs. hearts-and-minds) are each documented failures in Algeria. The finding: **tactical competence can't fix a legitimacy deficit**.
- **Lesson:** for the game — cells make decapitation hard but *infiltration* is the counter (bleuite), and repression's moral cost is real but slow to arrive.

## Supporting cases (compact)

- **Sétif (May 8, 1945)**: French massacre of Algerian VE-Day demonstrators (6,000+ killed by moderate estimates) — the atrocity-as-recruitment event that incubated the FLN generation. Repression as delayed-action radicalizer.
- **Viet Minh village committees**: parallel governance in liberated zones — taxation, justice, education — the shadow state as durable org infrastructure under occupation.
- **Swadeshi (India)**: economic self-reliance as mass campaign — bonfires of British cloth, indigenous production; boycott-as-economics preceding the Salt March's boycott-as-symbol.
- **Maroon communities / Palmares (Brazil, ~1605–1694)**: a parallel free state of escaped slaves surviving ~90 years against repeated military campaigns — the ultimate institutional anchoring case: durable because it *was* an alternative institution (agriculture, governance, military), not a protest.
- **Mau Mau**: see secret-societies doc — the oath as clandestine mass-binding technology, and the British counter (villagization = forced relocation as infrastructure warfare; counter-oathing "cleansing" ceremonies).

## Counter-Insurgency Doctrine (the enemy's textbook)

The anti-colonial wars produced the *formal theory* of counter-organizing:

1. **"Support of the population is not spontaneous; it must be acquired and organized"** (Galula, RAND) — counter-insurgency doctrine openly states that power must *do organizing too*. The counter-side of every campaign is a rival organizer.
2. **Quadrillage / population control**: census, ID systems, curfews, villagization — legibility projects (Scott's *Seeing Like a State*) as repression infrastructure.
3. **Infiltration > raids**: Algiers' second phase and bleuite showed surgical infiltration dismantles cells better than mass repression — and produces the intelligence to do it again.
4. **The torture trap**: tactical gains (intel, broken networks) vs strategic loss (legitimacy, radicalization, domestic opinion). Documented self-defeating loop.
5. **Hearts-and-minds** as the "clean" doctrine — and its documented inadequacy where the legitimacy deficit is structural (you can't out-organize people for the colonizer's own rule).

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Sacred/social binding infrastructure
Religious ceremony, oath-taking, ritual feasts as **commitment technology** that fuses trust faster than secular organizing — Bois Caïman in one night did what months of meetings do. Cost: the same sanctity makes heresy charges effective counter-attacks.

### Mechanic 2: Ostracism/boycott as insider-discipline action
The historical boycott's main target was the *defector* (farm-taker, scab, collaborator), not the boss. Mechanic: community can sanction members at low cost, high effectiveness — but requires dense network (reputation reach). Fails in sparse/mobile populations.

### Mechanic 3: Succession redundancy
Orgs can pre-build a shadow leadership structure (Ladies' Land League) that activates on decapitation. Investment: leadership capacity ×2 cost. Benefit: survives mass arrest events. Bonus realism: post-crisis, the redundant org may be *more militant* and resist being shut down.

### Mechanic 4: Counter-insurgency doctrine choice (power-holder)
AI power-holders pick a doctrine posture: **Mass repression** (fast network damage, radicalization debt accrues over time) / **Surgical infiltration** (slower, needs intel, high network damage, lower legitimacy cost — but scandal risk if exposed) / **Hearts-and-minds** (legitimacy repair — but fails if legitimacy deficit is structural/too deep). Each maps to documented outcomes.

### Mechanic 5: Repression's deferred debt
Atrocities generate `radicalization_debt` — recruits arrive years later (Sétif→FLN, Sharpeville→UDF). Power-holders see immediate suppression; the cost arrives on a delay. This is the mechanic version of "tactical win, strategic loss."
