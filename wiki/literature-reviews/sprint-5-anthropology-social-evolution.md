# Literature Review: Anthropology of Societal Development

**Sprint:** 5 — Anthropology (requested targeted research)
**Focus:** ecology → subsistence → violence → hierarchy → feudalism → revolt. The causal chain: hot/dry country produces herders; herders need protection from horse-riding spear-makers; that protection produces lords; lords stay until the peasants rise up.
**Date:** 2026-10-25

## The corpus

This is the actual research program for "how does a society form the way it does." The lit breaks into five links in the chain — each is a real body of anthropology/archaeology/history, and each maps onto mechanics we can build.

---

## Link 1: Ecology → subsistence (why shepherds?)

### Julian Steward — cultural ecology (Theory of Culture Change, 1955)
The founding claim: **subsistence technology + environment → social organization**. Groups exploiting similar environments with similar tools converge on similar social forms (Steward's "culture core"). Hot/dry steppe + domesticated herd animals → pastoralism → its characteristic organization (mobile camps, kin-based herds, raiding). *The game's ecology→subsistence→society pipeline is literally Steward's model.*

### Marvin Harris — cultural materialism (Cows, Pigs, Wars & Witches, 1974; Cannibals and Kings, 1977)
Radicalized Steward: infrastructure (demography/economy/tech) → structure (social organization) → superstructure (belief/ideology). Hot takes and all — sacred cows, pig taboos, warfare as protein regulation — Harris is the most mechanistic version of the argument: **material conditions generate the culture, and the culture rationalizes the material conditions after the fact.** Controversial but directly usable: the sim's ideology layer can literally be *generated by* the material layer.

### Jared Diamond — Guns, Germs & Steel (1997)
The popular synthesis: continental ecology (domesticable species, disease gradients, axis of spread) drives differential development. Not fashionable in academia (too deterministic, too ecological) — but as a *simulation model* it's exactly the right shape: input geography + species, output societal trajectory. For a game, Diamond is a design document.

### Robert Netting, Andrew Vayda — cultural ecology at household scale
Smallholders, Households (Netting, 1993) — the householder as the real economic strategist: labor allocation across years, risk management, the "household production unit" our `age-based-self-interest-economy.md` already models.

**Mechanic takeaway:** ecology template (climate/soil/water) → feasible subsistence modes (grain farming / pastoralism / horticulture / mixed) → each mode *generates* its social organization (mobility, property form, raiding norms, surplus depth).

## Link 2: Pastoralism & the steppe problem (herders vs mounted raiders)

### Anatoly Khazanov — Nomads and the Outside World (1984)
THE text on pastoral nomadism. Key claims:
- Pastoralism is **structurally dependent** on agriculture — nomads can't produce everything they need (grain, metal, textiles); they must trade or raid. The pastoral economy *requires* sedentary neighbors.
- Pastoral societies are **economically egalitarian but politically stratified** — everyone herds, but horses make wealth uneven and war leaders get followers.
- Steppe political forms oscillate: dispersed camps → warrior confederations → empires, driven by the sedentary relationship (when the sedentary state is weak, the steppe organizes; when strong, it fragments).

### David Anthony — The Horse, the Wheel, and Language (2007)
The archaeology of the Yamnaya: horse domestication (~3500 BCE on the Pontic steppe) + wheeled vehicles + mounted herding = the mobility revolution. Riding transformed pastoralism: herd size limits exploded, raiding range exploded, **the steppe became a threat generator for every agrarian society on its border**. Indo-European languages probably spread on horseback — the technology itself carried a whole cultural package (patriarchy, warrior aristocracy, sky-father religion).

### Thomas Barfield — The Perilous Frontier (1989)
Steppe empires (Xiongnu → Mongols) as *shadow empires*: they organize at the scale needed to extort the adjacent agrarian empire. The steppe empire is a protection racket at continental scale — **the mounted raider economy is a political form, not just a menace.**

### Azar Gat — War in Human Civilization (2006); Lawrence Keeley — War Before Civilization (1996)
The empirical check on "peaceful primitives": pre-state warfare was frequent and deadly (~25% of male deaths in some archaeological samples). Violence isn't a state invention — states *monopolize* it. Keeley's data is the honest baseline: villagers live with endemic low-level warfare (raids, feuds, ambushes) — the sim's raids/feuds should be **normal background noise, not exceptional events.**

**Mechanic takeaway:** pastoral regions on the map are mobile, horse-advantaged, trade-or-raid dependent, and dangerous to agrarian neighbors. When the border state is weak → raids; when a steppe war-leader unifies → extortion confederation; when the agrarian state is strong → the steppe fragments into trading herders.

## Link 3: Violence → hierarchy (protection becomes lordship)

### Elman Service — Primitive Social Organization (1962) / Origins of the State and Civilization (1975)
The classic evolutionary sequence: band → tribe → chiefdom → state. Key concept: the **chiefdom** — centralized leadership without true coercion yet; the chief *redistributes* (generosity legitimates rank). Feudalism is downstream of chiefdoms.

### Johnson & Earle — The Evolution of Human Societies (1987, 2000)
The modern version of the same: subsistence intensification → population pressure → **warfare → managerial hierarchies**. Critically, they document the *feedback*: more people need coordination, coordination concentrates power, concentrated power enables more extraction.

### The circumscription theory (Robert Carneiro, 1970)
States form where arable land is **bounded** (valley, oasis, island): population pressure + warfare + nowhere to flee → losers submit rather than migrate → stratification. *If escape is possible, hierarchy can't consolidate* — which is why frontiers/steepe/mountains resist states (see Scott below).

### The protection-racket model (Charles Tilly — "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime," 1985)
States are the successful protection rackets: **someone who can hurt you offers to protect you — from himself and others.** War making → state making → extraction → protection, in a feedback loop. The lord's castle is a protection service with a monopoly on force. This is the theoretical core of "shepherds need protection → get feudalism": the protector *becomes* the ruler.

### The mounted-warrior → aristocracy link
Across Eurasia the pattern repeats: whoever monopolizes expensive military tech (chariot, then horse+armor, then knight+lance) becomes the ruling class, because protecting-against-raiders requires *matching* the raiders' mobility. Military tech cost ↑ → warrior class stratification ↑ → feudal contract (land-for-service). Lynn White's controversial but mechanically suggestive "stirrup thesis" (Medieval Technology and Social Change, 1962): the stirrup made the heavy cavalryman, the heavy cavalryman made the knight, the knight made feudalism. Historians dispute the monocausality; for a game, **tech → military elite → political form** is exactly the dependency chain we want.

**Mechanic takeaway:** threat level + military-tech cost → protection market. A villager can't fight horsemen alone; someone who can (fortified, horse-equipped, spear-and-shield retinue) sells protection for dues. Feudalism = the protection market frozen into hereditary custom.

## Link 4: Feudalism as a stable (but not eternal) state

### Marc Bloch — Feudal Society (1939-40)
THE foundational text — feudalism not as a legal abstraction but as a **total social condition**: after Carolingian collapse, public power fragmented into personal bonds (vassalage), protection privatized, and the peasant's subjection became hereditary. Bloch's crucial insight: feudalism is what you get when **central authority fails and security must be bought locally** — the manor is a survival technology.

### Why feudalism persists (the stability question the user raises)
- **Fragmentation of sovereignty** — no single actor can change the rules; everyone is locked into reciprocal obligations (the contract web in `legal-framework-contracts.md`)
- **Custom as ratchet** — obligations become "the old way," and customary law is nearly impossible to legislate against
- **The Church legitimizes** — the estate system is cosmology (three orders: those who pray, fight, work)
- **Free peasant communes decay** — without protection they either submit to a lord or are conquered

### James C. Scott — The Art of Not Being Governed (2009); Against the Grain (2017)
The counterpoint: states are *fragile* because grain states need concentrated, legible, taxable subjects — and people keep **running away to the hills**. Zomia (upland SE Asia) as a zone of *deliberate* state-evasion: swidden farming, mobile settlement, heterodox religion — all chosen to stay ungovernable. Feudalism "stays" only where flight is impossible (circumscription again!). *For the game: the border/highland regions should be the zone where the feudal ratchet fails — pastoralists, outlaws, and escapees hold out there.*

### Rodney Hilton — Bond Men Made Free (1973); the Brenner Debate
Hilton's Marxist medieval history: feudalism's motor is **class conflict over surplus** — peasants resist extraction (commutation of labor dues, flight, revolt), and the lord's counter-pressure is what makes the system dynamic rather than static. The Brenner debate (1970s-80s, collected as "The Brenner Debate" 1985): the transition to capitalism was *driven by* peasant-class victories/losses — where peasants won freedom (France), smallholding persisted; where lords won (east of the Elbe), serfdom intensified; where lords won land but peasants kept mobility (England), agrarian capitalism. **The fate of feudalism is decided by peasant resistance outcomes — a game mechanic, not a backdrop.**

**Mechanic takeaway:** feudalism = equilibrium with a pressure gradient. It destabilizes on: (a) subsistence floor breach (Scott's moral economy), (b) peasant escape routes (uncircumscribed land), (c) elite succession crisis, (d) new military tech that devalues the knight (the historical gunpowder answer), (e) market/commutation options that let peasants buy out of dues.

## Link 5: The revolt (when the peasants rise)

### The revolt corpus
- **1381 English Rising (Wat Tyler):** poll tax breach of the moral economy + post-plague labor leverage → coordinated revolt. Fascinating detail: the rebels *negotiated* — they had a political program (end villeinage), not just rage.
- **German Peasants' War (1524-25):** the Twelve Articles — a *reform program* grounded in customary rights and scripture; crushed at Frankenhausen (~100k dead). The pattern: revolt articulates **restoration of just custom**, not utopia — rebels claim the lord broke the old contract first.
- **Peter Blickle — Communal Reformation:** the peasants weren't a mob; village *communes* (Gemeinden) had real organization — assemblies, elected headmen, customary law literacy. The revolt's infrastructure was the commune itself. **Organizing capacity pre-exists the revolt** — your `community.rs` mechanics are literally the precondition.
- **James Scott — Weapons of the Weak:** before the revolt, resistance is everyday — foot-dragging, pilfering, false compliance, gossip. Open revolt is the *failure mode* of everyday resistance, not its replacement.

### Mobilization theory (Charles Tilly — From Mobilization to Revolution, 1978)
Collective action = interest + organization + mobilization + opportunity. Grievance alone never produces revolt — you need **organization (commune/brotherhood/sect), resources, and an opening** (elite split, war defeat, fiscal crisis). Revolt is rare *because* it's a compound event — the sim should make it likewise.

### Relative deprivation (Ted Gurr — Why Men Rebel, 1970)
Rebellion correlates not with absolute misery but with the **gap between expectation and delivery** — post-plague peasants expecting better wages got the Statute of Labourers instead; *that* gap produced 1381, not poverty per se.

**Mechanic takeaway:** revolt = grievance × organization × opportunity. The commune provides organization; moral-economy breach provides grievance; succession crisis/war/famine provides opportunity. And everyday resistance (effort decay, petty theft, tax evasion, gossip/reputation damage) is the *default* mode — open revolt only when the channels close.

## Link 6 (the counter-argument): The Dawn of Everything — Graeber & Wengrow (2021)

The recent blockbuster that argues **the whole ladder is wrong**: societies didn't march band→tribe→chiefdom→state; they *chose* and *reversed* structures constantly. Seasonal alternation between hierarchy and egalitarianism (G&W's core evidence), cities without kings, deliberate egalitarian experiments. Three freedoms: to move away, to disobey, to reorganize.

For the game this is gold: **don't hardcode the ladder.** Ecology and military tech *bias* the trajectory (pastoral raiding → protection demand → stratification is a real pressure gradient) but the transition points are *contingent* — a village that keeps an escape route open, or a commune that wins early, doesn't feudalize at all. The ladder should be a *probability surface over historical paths*, not a tech-tree progression.

## Synthesis for game2 — the societal-evolution state machine

```
ecology template ──► subsistence mode ──► social organization seed
      │                    │
      │              pastoral + horse   →  raid pressure (constant)
      │                    │
      ▼                    ▼
   agrarian village  +  threat level  →  protection market
                                           │
                              protector monopolizes force
                                           │
                                           ▼
                                   stratification → feudal equilibrium
                                           │
              breach points: subsistence floor / escape route /
              elite crisis / military-tech shift / commutation
                                           │
                                           ▼
                        everyday resistance → organizing → REVOLT
                                           │
                                    outcomes: crushed / bargained /
                                    escape (new frontier village) /
                                    capture the manor (new elite)
```

**Every arrow is a mechanic we already have or have specced:** ecology→subsistence (world gen + tech), raiding (military), protection market (contracts + delegated agency), stratification (class/status layer), moral-economy floor (legitimacy register), organizing (community.rs), everyday resistance (effort/pilfering/gossip), revolt (collective action event), and the frontier escape (ungoverned map edges).
