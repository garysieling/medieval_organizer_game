# Lit Review - Ink Making: The Oil-Boiler's Fire

**Survey Date:** 2026-10-31
**Agent:** Literature Survey Agent (hot-pursuit, requested by Gary)
**Confidence:** High
**Domain:** History of Technology / Medieval Crafts / Printing Ink

## Summary

Gary remembered a medieval practice: boil a cauldron of linseed oil as a
precursor to ink making, dip bread in it (it fries), then mix in something
black to get oil-based ink. **This is real.** The trade was the
**oil-boiler / varnish-maker**: linseed oil heated for hours in a cauldron
over an open fire until it thickened into "varnish" (what chemistry calls
heat-bodied or stand oil). The bread is real too - period recipes call for
throwing a pound of bread crusts (and a dozen onions) into the boiling oil
to "degrease" it. The black pigment was not ash but **lampblack** - soot
collected from burning pitch, resin, or oil in a closed chamber. Varnish +
lampblack + a grinding stone = the oil-based printing ink that made
Gutenberg's press work, since water-based scribal inks would not wet metal
type.

The fire risk is also real and is the reason this happened **outside the
walls**: boiling oil ignites easily, boils over, and produces acrolein
fumes, so town bylaws and common sense pushed the craft into open spaces
away from buildings.

## The Historical Process

1. **The oil.** Linseed oil (pressed flax seed - the same plant that gives
   linen) is poured into a cauldron with plenty of headroom, because it
   swells as it heats.
2. **The boil.** Heated for hours over a moderate, enclosed fire. Cennini
   (1390s) insists on a little stove with a round opening the pot fits
   exactly "so that the flame cannot come up past it; because the flame
   would be glad to get to it, and you would jeopardize the oil, and also
   risk burning down the house." Oil reduced to about half = done.
3. **The degreasing (the bread part).** The Encyclopedie recipe: once the
   oil is hot, "throw into it a pound of bread crusts and a dozen onions,
   which speed up the degreasing of the oil." The crusts fry in the oil
   exactly as remembered.
4. **Deliberate ignition.** Period practice often *required* the hot oil's
   vapours to catch fire ("as will happen"), then be smothered with wet
   linen or a metal lid - burning off impurities. A red-hot poker was the
   traditional lighter. This is the moment it most often went wrong.
5. **The readiness test.** Drip oil from the iron ladle onto slate or tile;
   if it cools sticky "like thin glue," it is no longer oil - it is varnish.
6. **The black.** Lampblack made by burning pitch/resin (or lamp oil) in a
   "sac a noir" - a small sealed chamber lined with taut sheepskins. The
   soot deposits on the skins; you beat the outside with sticks and collect
   the black that falls. (The simpler version, candle soot on a basin, is
   already in our writing-materials review.)
7. **The ink.** Lampblack ground into the varnish on a muller stone.
   Gutenberg's ink (c.1450) analyzed as lampblack + linseed and walnut oil +
   pine resin + metal salts (driers) - the recipe stayed a trade secret
   ~200 years.

## Why Outside the Walls

- **Cennini:** the flame "would be glad to get to it... risk burning down
  the house."
- **Encyclopedie:** "For safety it is prudent when making the varnish to
  choose a wide space such as a garden and not to be near a building."
- **Fumes:** heating oil releases acrolein - "powerful and disagreeable
  smell, intense action upon the eyes" (Henley's); attendants needed a
  strong draught.
- **Town bylaws:** Pistoia's statute banished all crafts "from which
  stench arises" beyond the walls "whence stench cannot reach" citizens;
  English towns regulated furnace/oven crafts as fire risks and penalized
  those responsible for fires. Boil-over was a known killer - later rigs
  put the fire on a wheeled trolley so it could be yanked from under the
  pan.

## Key Sources

### Cennino Cennini, *Il Libro dell'Arte* ch. XCI (c.1390s)
**Rating:** A+ (primary source, the craftsman's own handbook)
**Key findings:** Boil 1-4 lb linseed oil in a glazed casserole on an
enclosed stove; flame must not reach the oil; reduce by half; result used
for mordants and tempering colors.

### Encyclopedie (Diderot & d'Alembert), "Encre noir" (1751-72)
**Rating:** A (primary technical encyclopedia, detailed trade recipe)
**Key findings:** Printing ink = varnish (heated oil) + black from burnt
resin; boil ~2h, oil ignites and is smothered with wet linen; throw in 1 lb
bread crusts + a dozen onions to degrease; slate-drip readiness test;
"choose a wide space such as a garden and not near a building"; the
sac-a-noir soot chamber described in full.

### NCBI - *Printing Processes and Printing Inks* (book chapter)
**Rating:** A (peer-reviewed/conservation science)
**Key findings:** Gutenberg's ink was oil-based because aqueous gum inks
didn't wet metal type; lampblack + heated linseed/nut oil + rosin + metal
salt driers; heating oils was "a significant hazard to ink makers (fires,
vapours, spills)"; some recipes required ignition then extinguishing with a
metal cover.

### Savage, Stiber & Morenus - *Pre-Industrial Western Printing Inks, c.1450-1850* (SAS-Space)
**Rating:** A (most substantial scholarly survey of the material)
**Key findings:** Ink formulation varied widely across the handpress period;
trade secrecy kept recipes unpublished until ~17th c.; English vs Dutch
varnish practice differed (Dutch pure linseed + little rosin).

### "The Path to Pistoia" - *Past & Present* 246 (OUP)
**Rating:** A (peer-reviewed journal, urban environmental history)
**Key findings:** Pistoia statute: no artisan may practise a stench-craft
inside the walls - "he must carry out such craft and labour beyond the
city's walls."

### Fire Prevention in Late Medieval British Towns (Oxford Academic)
**Rating:** A
**Key findings:** Towns regulated combustible materials and furnace crafts;
fire-fighting bylaws + harsh penalties for those deemed responsible.

### NIST Bureau of Standards Circular 53 + Henley's Formulas
**Rating:** B/C (older technical references)
**Key findings:** "Long" vs "short" varnish by dripping thread; oil heated
to point where vapours take fire, red-hot poker to start it; boil-over and
acrolein hazards; fire-on-rails contrivance.

## Correction to the Remembered Recipe

The black was **soot (lampblack), not ash** - ash is the pale mineral
residue and would make a grey wash at best. The intuition "something burnt
goes in" is right; it's the smoke of the fire, not what's left at the
bottom. (Wood ash did have a medieval use near this trade though: potash
for lye and soap.)

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: The Oil-Boiler's Visit (periodic event)
**Academic Basis:** Itinerant/specialist varnish-makers; bylaws pushed the
craft outside walls.
**Translation:**
- A travelling ink-maker walks the road in every ~year (like the circus),
  and pitches a cauldron on the meadow outside the gate - because that's
  where the law and the fire risk put him.
- Multi-day process, one phase per tick-day: set up → boil → bread &
  onions (a villager brings crusts; they come back fried - a small joy) →
  lampblack stirred in → sells `ink` before leaving.
- Variables: boil_days, fire_chance_per_day, ink_yield.

### Mechanic 2: The Oil Can Catch (fire spread)
**Academic Basis:** Ignition was routine and sometimes catastrophic;
medieval towns burned down around trades like this.
**Translation:**
- Small chance per boiling day the cauldron flares. Fire becomes burning
  map tiles that spread to flammable neighbours (grass, wheat, trees,
  buildings), blocked by water/road, burning out to scorched ground that
  slowly recovers - a simple CA on the real TileMap (emergence.rs proved
  the pattern).
- Villagers near the fire flee or complain; if it reaches a building,
  stock burns. Worst case is rare but memorable - "the year the meadow
  burned."

### Mechanic 3: Acrolein (the stench grievance)
**Academic Basis:** Noxious fumes were half the reason for zoning.
**Translation:** Villagers near the cauldron during the boil get the
existing `Problem::Smelly` grievance - the perfumer's trade exists because
of smells like this one.

### Mechanic 4: Ink Independence
**Academic Basis:** Printing ink was a distinct oil-based product bought
from ink-makers, not made by printers.
**Translation:** `ink` is currently import-only (trader exotics). The
boiler's visit stocks the print shop directly - a local source for an
import, and a nice economic beat: the printer watches the smoke over the
meadow like everyone else.

### Mechanic 5 (stretch): Varnish for the Painter
**Academic Basis:** The same boiled oil is the binder of oil paint.
**Translation:** leftover varnish could feed `Vocation::Painter` (oil
painting unlock) in a later change.
