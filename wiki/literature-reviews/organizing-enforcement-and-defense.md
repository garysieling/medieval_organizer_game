# Lit Review: The Enforcement Problem — How Wins Are Defended (and Lost)

**Survey Date:** 2026-10-31
**Agent:** Literature Survey Agent
**Domain:** Social Movement History / Post-Victory Dynamics
**Series:** Organizing research round 2 (user-requested). Builds on [organizing-historical-campaigns.md](organizing-historical-campaigns.md) and [organizing-countertactics.md](organizing-countertactics.md)
**Confidence:** High on the pattern; the individual cases are well-documented history

## Purpose

Round-1 synthesis flagged **"victory-then-disband"** as a top-3 failure mode. This survey examines it directly: **what happens after the win**. The finding is stark — winning and keeping are different games, and movements systematically under-invest in the second. Losers, by contrast, reorganize. The asymmetry between demobilizing winners and re-mobilizing losers may be the single most important dynamic the campaign model is missing.

## The Core Asymmetry

> **Movements disband after winning. Losers reorganize after losing.**

Every case below shows the same shape: victory triggers demobilization (energy spent, purpose achieved, funders leave, leaders exit to institutions) while the defeated side *retains its organization* — because defeat is also mobilizing, and the institutions that opposed the change (businesses, churches, party machinery, state agencies) don't dissolve when they lose a campaign. They wait.

## Cases

### Reconstruction → Redemption (1865–1877+) — the catastrophic case
- Won: Emancipation, 13th/14th/15th Amendments, Freedmen's Bureau, Black elected officials across the South.
- Lost: The enforcement coalition dissolved. Federal troops withdrawn 1877 (Compromise of 1877). The counter-movement (Klan, White Leagues, "redeemer" Democrats) had *never* disbanded — it was organized, armed, and waiting. Within ~30 years: disenfranchisement, Jim Crow, convict leasing.
- **Lesson:** Wins dependent on an occupying enforcement presence evaporate when it leaves. Nothing replaced troops with *institutions owned by the beneficiaries* — the Freedmen's Bureau was always temporary by design.

### Civil rights → voting rights erosion (1965–present)
- Won: VRA 1965 with preclearance — an *enforcement mechanism built into the win* (the strongest possible form).
- Lost (partially): Shelby County v. Holder 2013 gutted preclearance; within hours states moved laws that had been blocked for decades. The 48-year gap shows both that enforcement worked while it lasted AND that the counter-movement litigated patiently for half a century.
- **Lesson:** The win that builds enforcement into itself is the strongest form — but enforcement institutions are themselves attack surface, requiring permanent defense (litigation infrastructure, watchdogs).

### UFW (1975–1990s)
- Won: ALRA 1975 — first farmworker bargaining law; union contracts through the 70s.
- Lost: Governor Deukmejian's administration defanged the ALRB (enforcement agency captured by the regulated side — a distinct failure mode). Combined with Chavez's internal purges, membership collapsed from ~100k to under 10k. Contracts lapsed and were never replaced.
- **Lesson:** Winning the *agency* isn't the end — agencies get captured. Enforcement requires the organized constituency to remain organized *and* watch the watchers.

### Suffrage (1920–1965)
- Won: 19th Amendment. Immediately: NAWSA dissolved into the League of Women Voters — a service org, deliberately non-militant.
- Lost: The coalition fragmented (protective labor law vs equal-rights split); ERA failed; Black women's actual disenfranchisement continued unchallenged for 45 years — the "win" was never enforced for the coalition's most vulnerable.
- **Lesson:** Institutionalizing as a *service* org (education, registration) rather than a *power* org (enforcement, confrontation) is the classic post-victory trap. It preserves the brand and surrenders the leverage.

### Labor (1935–1981+)
- Won: Wagner Act 1935, mass industrial unionism, ~35% density by 1950s.
- Lost incrementally: Taft-Hartley 1947 (right-to-work, secondary boycott bans), employer union-avoidance industry, PATCO 1981 (Reagan fired strikers — demonstrated that striking public unions could be broken), private-sector density now ~6%.
- **Lesson:** The counter-attack took 46 years of patient legal/political work. Counter-movements play long games; movements plan in campaign cycles.

### Roe (1973–2022) — the counter-case that proves it
- The anti-abortion side *never demobilized*. It built a 49-year enforcement machine: single-issue voting blocs, Federalist Society judicial pipeline, state-level incremental restrictions (TRAP laws, Hyde), crisis pregnancy centers as parallel institutions, march-for-life as recurring structure test.
- Dobbs 2022 was a *counter-movement* victory — arguably the most successful organized counter-organizing campaign in US history.
- **Lesson:** The loser of 1973 ran the playbook better than the winner did. This is the strongest evidence that post-victory organizing determines outcomes more than the victory itself.

### Keystone XL → ISDS counter-attack (2021–22)
- Won: pipeline cancelled after a decade of coalition organizing.
- Counter-attacked: TC Energy sued the US for $15B under NAFTA's investor-state dispute mechanism (case eventually dropped/settled 2023 after renegotiation removed the claim basis — but the *attempt* shows the pattern: wins get counter-sued).
- **Lesson:** Modern wins attract legal counter-attacks (see also Energy Transfer v. Greenpeace in the counter-playbook doc). Legal defense capacity is now part of holding a win.

### Disability rights (1977–1990+) — the positive case
- Won: 504 regulations 1977. The movement did NOT disband — kept litigating, expanded demands, won ADA 1990 (Capitol Crawl as the structure test).
- Partial: ADA enforcement remains plaintiff-driven (private litigation model) — meaning enforcement depends on individual capacity to sue; accessibility remains contested decades later.
- **Lesson:** Sustained org between wins is possible and produces compounding wins — but enforcement-by-litigation is a weak institutional form vs a dedicated agency.

## Failure Modes of Post-Victory Periods

1. **Demobilization on signature** — treating the law/deal as the end rather than the start of enforcement
2. **Service-org conversion** — power org → educational/charitable org (suffrage, some labor internationals)
3. **Agency capture** — the enforcing body gets captured by the regulated side (ALRB, NLRB cycles)
4. **Counter-movement re-organization** — losers keep their institutions and patiently litigate/legislate for decades
5. **Legal counter-attack** — ISDS suits, constitutional challenges, preemption
6. **Coalition dissolution** — the big-tent alliance needed to win can't hold post-win (interest divergence)
7. **Generational forgetting** — the win becomes "how things are" and the constituency never learns it was fought for

## What Defended Wins (the counter-pattern)

- **Enforcement designed into the win** (VRA preclearance — the strongest form; survived 48 years)
- **Parallel institutions that outlast the campaign** (union shops, movement schools like Highlander, mutual aid, church networks)
- **Watchdog/oversight function** (NLG observers, treaty-monitoring orgs, compliance journalism)
- **Second-win readiness** — campaigns sequenced so one win funds the next (disability 504→ADA)
- **The counter-movement model itself** — Roe opponents built for permanence; movements can learn their own lesson back

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Win-states are not end-states
Every victory spawns an **enforcement phase**: the win decays over time unless maintained. `win_integrity` drains by default; maintenance actions (watchdog committees, legal defense, compliance checks, recurring structure tests) restore it. Losers retain org capacity and run counter-campaigns on a delay timer.

### Mechanic 2: Enforcement forms (strength tiers)
| Form | Strength | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Norm/culture | Weak | "Everyone knows better now" — decays in a generation |
| Private litigation | Medium | ADA plaintiffs — expensive, individual |
| Agency | Medium-strong | NLRB/ALRB — powerful but capturable |
| Self-executing legal mechanism | Strong | VRA preclearance-style automatic checks |
| Beneficiary-owned institution | Strongest | Unions, cooperatives, endowments — the win IS an org |

### Mechanic 3: Counter-movement timer
After any major player win, the defeated faction enters a "regroup" state: it retains (maybe grows) its base, runs long-cycle counter-campaigns (litigation, legislation, elections). Player sees a slow-burn threat meter, not a defeated faction. This is the Reconstruction mechanic.

### Mechanic 4: Demobilization temptation
Post-win, the game offers tempting off-ramps: "declare victory" events, leaders get institutional job offers, funders propose converting to a charity. All are real historical dynamics — and each is a trap the player can fall into or refuse.

### Mechanic 5: Watchdog role
A new org function distinct from organizer/leader: **watchdog** — monitors compliance, flags violations, raises alarm when enforcement drifts. Costs capacity to staff; prevents silent decay of win_integrity.
