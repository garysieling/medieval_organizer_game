## 1. Writing Surfaces: From Animal Skins to Paper

### 1.1 Vellum and Parchment Production

**Primary Sources:** Traditional parchment making (multiple historical sources), Archives.gov standards, Medieval workshop manuals

#### Historical Background

Vellum is distinguished from parchment either by being made from calfskin (higher quality) rather than the skin of other animals, or simply by being of superior quality. Calf, sheep, and goat were most commonly used, with pig, deer, donkey, horse, and camel used occasionally in different regions.

#### The Production Process

The traditional parchment making process involved seven critical stages:

**Stage 1: Selection and Initial Soaking**
Goat, sheep, and calf skins are selected at abattoirs for quality. Fresh skins are immediately soaked in water to prevent decomposition and prepare for liming.

**Stage 2: Liming (8-14 days)**
The skin is immersed in a lime bath (strong alkaline solution) which:
- Dissolves hair roots and epidermis
- Loosens the grain layer
- Swells the collagen fibers
- Expands hair follicles for easier removal

**Stage 3: Hair Removal (Beaming)**
Once ready, skins are placed over a beamer (curved wooden bench) and a special knife called a scudder is used to scrape away the loosened hair.

**Stage 4: Stretching**
The cleaned skin is mounted on a wooden stretching frame with tension adjusted continually during the scraping process.

**Stage 5: Scraping**
A razor-sharp lunar knife (curved crescent blade) is used to scrape the stretched skin, creating an even thickness while ensuring the grain runs in the same direction throughout.

**Stage 6: Drying**
Skins remain on their frames while drying under controlled tension. The tension during drying is critical to the final quality.

**Stage 7: Finishing**
To even the texture and create a smooth writing surface, the dried skin is scoured with fine sand or powdered limestone (pumice-like). A red clay called Armenian bole may be applied in preparation for later gilding.

#### Quality Hierarchy

- **Vellum (highest quality)**: Calfskin, very thin, smooth, suitable for fine manuscripts
- **Parchment (medium quality)**: Sheepskin, goatskin, general purpose writing
- **Utility grades**: Pigskin, deerskin, coarser uses

### 1.2 Papyrus Making (Ancient Egyptian Technique)

**Primary Sources:** Science Buddies papyrus project, Historical making guides, Ancient Egyptian evidence

#### Process Overview

Papyrus is a thick, paper-like material produced from the pith of the *Cyperus papyrus* plant, used in ancient Egypt and Mediterranean societies long before Chinese paper reached Europe.

#### Manufacturing Steps

**Step 1: Harvesting**
Cut the *Cyperus papyrus* plant stalks, selecting mature plants with thick piths.

**Step 2: Pith Extraction**
Cut thin ribbon-like strips from the pith (interior) of the plant stalk.

**Step 3: First Layer**
Lay out strips side-by-side to make a sheet, with strips touching edge-to-edge.

**Step 4: Second Layer**
Place a second layer on top with strips running perpendicular (90 degrees) to the first layer.

**Step 5: Pounding**
Pound the two layers together using a mallet or smooth stone, causing the plant sugars and moisture to bind the layers into a unified sheet.

**Step 6: Pressing and Drying**
Press under weight while drying to create a smooth, flat surface suitable for writing.

### 1.3 Vegetable Papyrus (Plant Fiber Papers)

**Primary Sources:** Handpapermaking.org vegetable papyrus research, Colorado Gardener plant fiber studies

#### Historical Context

A similar technique to Egyptian papyrus can be applied to many plant materials. In Mesoamerica around 100 AD, Mayans developed a thin sheet mat for bookmaking called 'amatl' in Nahuatl, produced by boiling and pounding the inner bark of the Ficus tree.

#### Modern Rediscovery: Vegetables

Interestingly, vegetable papyrus can be made using common vegetables that are firm when raw but soften when cooked: **zucchini, peppers, onions, beets, carrots, parsnips, squash, and turnips** all work.

#### Process

1. Slice vegetables thinly (uniform thickness)
2. Cook until tender but not mushy
3. Arrange slices in overlapping layers (perpendicular orientation)
4. Press heavily to remove moisture and bond layers
5. Dry under continued pressure

The resulting vegetable papyrus reveals intricate and beautiful patterns from the cellular structure of the plants. This is a fragile, decorative writing surface rather than archival quality, but demonstrates the versatility of plant fiber papermaking.

#### Bark Papers (Amate)

In Mexico and Central America, a thick, dark bark paper known as amate is made from trees in the fig family (*Ficus* sp.) and often brightly painted with birds and flowers. The inner bark is boiled until soft, then beaten with smooth stones until it forms a felted mat.

### 1.4 European Linen Rag Paper (Water-Based Papermaking)

**Primary Sources:** Drew University papermaking archives, UCSB ballad archive, Medieval European paper history

#### Historical Development

Paper was introduced to Europe from the Islamic world around the 12th century, with the first paper mills established in Spain and Italy. By the late medieval period, paper began to compete with parchment for non-archival uses due to lower cost.

#### The Raw Material: Linen and Cotton Rags

Paper was made from **linen rags** (primarily) and cotton collected from recycled clothing. Old shirts, undergarments, and fabric scraps were valuable commodities for papermakers.

#### The Vatman Process

The medieval European papermaking process involved specialized roles and equipment:

**Stage 1: Rag Preparation**
Rags were sorted by quality and color, dampened, and left to rot for 4-5 days to begin breaking down the fibers.

**Stage 2: Stamping Mill**
Rotting rags were placed in a water-powered stamping mill which pounded them into a pulp of long fibers. The stamping mill used large wooden hammers lifted by a waterwheel and dropped repeatedly onto the rags.

**Stage 3: The Vat**
Pulp was transferred into a large vat (typically about 330 gallons) which was kept agitated and warm to maintain an even suspension of fibers.

**Stage 4: The Mould and Deckle**
The **vatman** used two key tools:
- **Mould**: An oblong rectangular wire sieve mounted on a wood frame, with chainlines (wires running across the short end) and a woven mesh of smaller wires
- **Deckle**: A removable wooden rim fitted onto the mould to create a tray-like sieve with raised edges, controlling the sheet size

**Stage 5: Sheet Formation**
The vatman dipped the mould (with deckle attached) into the vat so that the pulp-solution drained through the wire mesh, leaving a layer of matted fibers as water drained away. A skilled shake distributed the fibers evenly.

**Stage 6: Couching**
The **coucher** removed the deckle and rolled the newly-made wet sheet onto a piece of felt. Sheets were stacked with felt interleaving.

**Stage 7: Pressing**
Stacks of sheets were placed in a heavy press to squeeze out excess water.

**Stage 8: Drying**
Sheets were hung to air dry, then often sized with gelatin to reduce absorbency and prevent ink from feathering.

#### Quality Markers

- **Laid lines**: Visible lines from the mould wires (chainlines and laid lines)
- **Deckle edge**: Irregular feathered edge characteristic of handmade paper
- **Watermarks**: Wire designs incorporated into the mould that leave translucent marks

## 2. Inks: From Iron Gall to Berry Juice

### 2.1 Iron Gall Ink - The Medieval Standard

**Primary Sources:** Medieval Codes (Oak Gall Ink), Wikipedia iron gall ink, SCA ink-making manuals

#### Historical Dominance

Iron gall ink was the standard ink formulation used in Europe for the 1400-year period between the 5th and 19th centuries. It was used for critically important documents including the Magna Carta and the U.S. Declaration of Independence.

#### Three-Ingredient Formula

Iron gall ink contains:
1. **Oak galls** (provide tannic acid)
2. **Ferrous sulphate** (copperas/vitriol - green crystals, provides iron)
3. **Gum arabic** (binder, from acacia tree sap)
4. Plus water as solvent

#### Historical Variations

Medieval formulas were less precise than later recipes. Some variations:
- Pieces of scrap iron dumped directly into the gall solution
- Wine and vinegar used alongside water as solvents
- Varying ratios of galls to vitriol

#### The Chemistry of Permanence

When the iron-tannin complex hits the page and oxidizes, it forms a compound that **bonds to the cellulose fibers** of parchment or paper, becoming part of the material rather than sitting on top of the surface. This creates exceptional permanence but can eventually cause the ink to corrode through the substrate.

#### Production Process

1. Crush oak galls (abnormal growths on oak trees caused by wasp larvae)
2. Soak crushed galls in water (or wine/vinegar) to extract tannic acid
3. Dissolve ferrous sulphate in the tannin solution
4. Add gum arabic dissolved in water as a binder
5. Age the ink (improves color and flow)

The ink appears pale blue-gray when first applied but darkens to black as it oxidizes.

### 2.2 Carbon Ink and Lampblack

**Primary Sources:** Textual Criticism lampblack guide, Burgundian Black digital manual, 1596 Dutch recipe

#### Historical Context

Carbon black ink recipes in the medieval period are less common than iron gall ink formulas. In Europe before 1600, carbon black ink was primarily used for outlining in illumination and making printing ink rather than main body text.

#### Carbon Sources

**Lampblack**: Soot collected from oil lamps or candles
**Charcoal**: Powdered burnt wood

The preparation for lampblack has been known since classical times: a flame from a lamp or candle is interrupted with a metal surface (basin, plate), and the carbon soot collects on the metal where it can be easily brushed off.

#### Simple Medieval Recipe (1596 Dutch)

"Take a wax candle, and light it, hold it under a cleane bason or a candlestick, till the smoke of the candle hangeth thereon, then put a little warm gum water into it, which tempered together will be a good inke."

#### Modern Reconstruction Recipe

- 30 ml water
- 3g gum arabic
- 3.67g lampblack

**Process:**
1. Dissolve gum arabic in water (10:1 water to gum ratio)
2. Incorporate lampblack into gum solution
3. Mix until homogeneous
4. Strain through cloth to remove large particles

Carbon ink has the advantage of being completely stable and non-corrosive, never eating through the substrate like iron gall ink can.

### 2.3 Berry Inks (Natural Dye Inks)

**Primary Sources:** Meadow Arts natural ink guides, Nikki Lynn Design elderberry ink, Revolutionary War pokeberry ink

#### Historical Usage

Elderberry ink was particularly popular in medieval manuscripts and was often used by scribes for creating texts and illustrations. During the Revolutionary War, soldiers made pokeberry ink to write letters home, demonstrating the accessibility of this technique.

#### Best Berries for Ink

- **Elderberries** (*Sambucus nigra*): Deep purple-black, excellent permanence
- **Blackberries** (*Rubus* spp.): Purple-black, good color
- **Pokeberries** (*Phytolacca americana*): Magenta-purple, vibrant but less permanent

#### Basic Berry Ink Recipe

**For Pokeberry Ink:**
1. Collect 2 cups ripe pokeweed berries
2. Rinse thoroughly
3. Crush with mortar and pestle
4. Strain to separate juice from seeds and skins (**dispose of poisonous seeds safely**)
5. Add 1 teaspoon white vinegar or isopropyl alcohol (prevents mold)

**For Elderberry Ink:**
1. Crush elderberries to extract juice
2. Strain out solids
3. For every cup of elderberry juice, add 1-2 teaspoons gum arabic (binder)
4. Add a small amount of vinegar to prevent mold growth

#### Limitations

Berry inks tend to fade over time, especially with light exposure. They were more commonly used for:
- Practice writing
- Temporary records
- Decorative purposes
- When other inks were unavailable

### 2.4 Mineral Pigment Inks (Ground Stone)

**Primary Sources:** Medieval Palette (Sacred Stone), SCA pigment-making manual, Webexhibits pigments through ages

#### The Stone Dust Connection

The user's insight about "stone dust from masons" is historically accurate! Medieval pigments were made by grinding colored minerals into fine powders, and stonemasons would have had access to colorful stone dust as a byproduct of their work.

#### Major Mineral Pigments

**Ochre Family** (iron oxide + clay + sand):
- **Yellow ochre**: Warm yellow-brown
- **Red ochre**: Rust red to brick red
- **Purple ochre**: Rare, violet-purple
- **Sienna**: Yellow-brown to reddish-brown
- **Umber**: Deep brown (iron oxide + manganese oxide)

Umber is considered one of the oldest pigments known to humans and was used extensively in the Middle Ages to create different shades of brown, most often for skin tones in illumination.

#### Grinding Process

The labor-intensive process of making pigments from minerals:

1. **Selection**: Find a rock with a desirable color
2. **Initial crushing**: Crush with a hammer until small enough to grind in a mortar
3. **Fine grinding**: Grind in a stone mortar with pestle until powder is achieved
4. **Final levigation**: Final grinding on a marble tile with a muller for extremely fine particles

Historians believe cave artists ground pigments in natural stone hollows in cave floors, where color stains have been observed. Shoulder bones of large animals, stained with color, were likely used as mortars for pigment grinding.

#### Converting Pigment to Ink

Pigments are opaque particles that need a carrier or binder to "glue" them in place. For manuscript work:

- **Glair** (egg white): Most common binder for manuscript illumination
- **Gum arabic**: Alternative binder, more glossy finish
- **Water**: Solvent for tempera-style paint

**Basic Recipe:**
1. Grind mineral to finest powder possible
2. Mix pigment powder with small amount of water
3. Add glair (beaten and settled egg white) or gum arabic solution
4. Mix thoroughly until smooth consistency
5. Test on scrap parchment and adjust thickness

Colored inks were generally made from mineral pigments including red ochre, umber, and yellow ochre for general writing, with more expensive pigments (lapis lazuli for ultramarine blue, minium/red lead, orpiment/yellow, verdigris/green) reserved for illumination.

### 2.5 Experimental: Burnt Linseed Oil Ink

**Note:** While burnt linseed oil is primarily known as a component of oil-based paints and printing inks (particularly after the development of printing presses in the 15th century), it could theoretically be used in ink formulations.

#### Linseed Oil Properties

Linseed oil is extracted from flax seeds (*Linum usitatissimum*) and has been used since ancient times as a drying oil. When heated or "burnt," it polymerizes faster, creating a thicker, stickier medium.

#### Hypothetical Ink Formulation

Burnt linseed oil could be combined with:
- Carbon black (lampblack or charcoal powder) for pigment
- Small amount of turpentine or alcohol for thinning
- Drying agents to accelerate setting

This would create an oil-based ink similar to early printing inks rather than traditional water-based writing inks. Such inks would be more suitable for printing blocks or very bold calligraphy rather than fine manuscript work.

## 3. Bookbinding: From Quires to Codex

**Primary Sources:** SCA Art of Bookbinding guide, Coptic binding research, Ethiopian binding comparison studies

### 3.1 The Evolution from Scroll to Codex

The codex (book form with bound pages) emerged in the early Christian period as an improvement over scrolls:
- Random access to any page
- Compact storage
- Both sides of the page usable
- Easier to reference multiple sections

### 3.2 Coptic Binding - The First True Codex

**Historical Context:**
Coptic binding comprises methods employed by early Christians in Egypt from as early as the 2nd century AD to the 11th century.

**Key Characteristics:**
Coptic bindings are characterized by sections sewn through their folds and attached to each other with chain stitch linkings across the spine. This creates an exposed spine with decorative sewing visible from the outside.

**Technical Details:**
- No adhesive used (entirely structural sewing)
- Wooden boards for covers
- Multiple-needle sewing technique
- Sewing stations on the spine fold
- Chain stitch links between quires

### 3.3 Medieval Quires and Gathering

**Quire Definition:**
A quire (also called a gathering) is a group of folios where their center folds are lined up with each other. The most common medieval quire was:
- **Quaternion**: 4 folded sheets = 8 leaves = 16 pages
- **Quinternion**: 5 folded sheets = 10 leaves = 20 pages
- **Sexternion**: 6 folded sheets = 12 leaves = 24 pages

### 3.4 Medieval European Binding Process

**Stage 1: Preparing Quires**
Folios are folded, nested, and marked for sewing stations. Signatures are pierced on the spine fold where sewing will pass through.

**Stage 2: Sewing the Text Block**
Medieval quires were typically sewn over raised cords or slit double cords. Common configurations:
- 2 sewing stations (simple books)
- 3 sewing stations (standard)
- 4-5 sewing stations (large books)

**Stage 3: Attaching Boards**
Wooden boards (oak, beech, or other hardwoods) were attached by lacing cords through channels in the boards or by lacing directly through holes.

**Stage 4: Covering**
Leather (often calfskin or goatskin) was stretched over the boards and adhered with wheat paste. The leather typically extended beyond the page edges to provide protection.

**Stage 5: Finishing**
- Clasps or ties to keep the book closed (parchment expands/contracts with humidity)
- Bosses (metal protrusions) to protect covers when laid flat
- Tooling and decoration on leather covers
- Corner pieces and edge protection

## 4. Manuscript Illumination

**Primary Sources:** Getty Museum manuscript making guide, Vanderbilt Book as Art materials, Wikipedia illuminated manuscripts

### 4.1 The Illumination Process Order

Critically, the **order of operations** in illumination was essential for best results:

1. **Prepare parchment surface** (pumicing, pouncing)
2. **Apply gesso** to areas that will receive gold leaf
3. **Apply gold leaf** before painting (must burnish after application)
4. **Burnish gold** with smooth stone or hound's tooth to make it reflective
5. **Apply painted illumination** (pigments in glair or gum arabic)
6. **Add fine details** and line work last

### 4.2 Gold Leaf Application

**Gesso Preparation:**
The areas to be illuminated were first painted with gesso (a mixture of plaster, white lead, glue, and sometimes sugar for stickiness). A red clay known as **Armenian bole** was sometimes added to the gesso, giving the gold greater warmth and luster.

**Gold Leaf Application:**
Sheets of gold leaf were placed over the sticky, wet gesso substance. The gold leaf adhered only to the wet gesso areas. Gold leaf is extremely thin (typically 0.1 micrometers) and delicate, requiring careful handling.

**Burnishing:**
The gold leaf was burnished (rubbed with a smooth stone or even a hound's tooth) to make it reflective and adhere permanently to the gesso. This created the characteristic brilliant shine of medieval manuscripts.

### 4.3 Pigments and Paint Preparation

Paint pigments came from multiple sources:

**Mineral Pigments:**
- **Minium** (red lead): Red-orange, from which the term 'miniature' derives
- **Lapis lazuli**: Crushed semiprecious stone, produces ultramarine blue (extremely expensive)
- **Orpiment**: Arsenic sulfide, produces yellow (toxic)
- **Verdigris**: Copper acetate, produces green (corrosive)
- **Ochres and umbers**: Earth pigments for browns and yellows

**Organic Pigments:**
- **Madder**: Root-derived red
- **Indigo**: Plant-derived blue
- **Saffron**: Expensive yellow from crocus stamens

**Binders:**
Powdered pigments were blended with:
- **Glair** (egg white): Most common, beaten until frothy then allowed to settle
- **Gum arabic**: Alternative, gives glossier finish
- Water as solvent

### 4.4 Types of Illumination

**Miniatures:**
'Miniature' refers to illuminated illustrations in fine manuscript books that are not connected to other parts of the book's decorative design (such as borders or initials). The term derives from 'minium' (red lead pigment), not from size.

**Historiated Initials:**
Large decorated initial letters containing pictorial scenes related to the text.

**Border Decoration:**
Elaborate decorative borders around text, often featuring:
- Acanthus leaves and vines
- Fantastical creatures
- Geometric patterns
- Heraldic devices

**Marginalia:**
Drawings in the margins (known as marginalia) allowed scribes to add their own notes, diagrams, translations, and even comic flourishes. Medieval marginalia is famous for bizarre and humorous content including:
- Knights fighting snails
- Rabbits hunting humans
- Obscene or scatological humor
- Political commentary

## 5. Game Mechanic Translations

### Mechanic 1: Writing Surface Production Chain

**System:** Multi-stage crafting with quality tiers and resource requirements

```rust
enum WritingSurface {
    Vellum { quality: VellumQuality, sheets: u32 },
    Parchment { quality: ParchmentQuality, sheets: u32 },
    Papyrus { sheets: u32 },
    VegetablePapyrus { plant_type: PlantType, sheets: u32, durability: f32 },
    LinenPaper { quality: PaperQuality, sheets: u32 },
}

enum VellumQuality {
    Utility,         // Rough parchment, cheap
    Standard,        // General purpose
    Fine,            // Smooth, even
    Exceptional,     // Calfskin vellum, illumination grade
}

struct ParchmentProduction {
    stage: ProductionStage,
    time_remaining: u32,  // In-game hours
    quality_roll: f32,    // 0.0-1.0, affected by crafter skill
}

enum ProductionStage {
    Soaking { days: u32 },
    Liming { days: u32 },         // 8-14 days
    HairRemoval,                   // Requires scudder tool
    Stretching,                    // Requires frame
    Scraping { skill_check: f32 }, // Critical for quality
    Drying { days: u32 },
    Finishing,                     // Pumice or sand finishing
}

impl WritingSurface {
    fn cost_to_produce(&self) -> ResourceCost {
        match self {
            WritingSurface::Vellum { quality, sheets } => {
                let base_cost = match quality {
                    VellumQuality::Utility => 5,
                    VellumQuality::Standard => 10,
                    VellumQuality::Fine => 20,
                    VellumQuality::Exceptional => 50,
                };
                ResourceCost {
                    calfskin: *sheets,
                    lime: *sheets / 10,
                    labor_hours: *sheets * base_cost,
                    tools: vec![Tool::Scudder, Tool::LunarKnife, Tool::StretchingFrame],
                }
            },
            WritingSurface::LinenPaper { quality, sheets } => {
                ResourceCost {
                    linen_rags: *sheets * 2,   // 2 rags per sheet
                    water: *sheets * 10,        // Vatman process water-intensive
                    labor_hours: *sheets * 3,   // Faster than parchment
                    tools: vec![Tool::Mould, Tool::Deckle, Tool::Press],
                }
            },
            WritingSurface::VegetablePapyrus { plant_type, sheets, .. } => {
                ResourceCost {
                    vegetables: *sheets * 10,   // Many vegetable slices per sheet
                    labor_hours: *sheets * 2,
                    tools: vec![Tool::Knife, Tool::Pot, Tool::Press],
                }
            },
            _ => ResourceCost::default(),
        }
    }

    fn production_time_days(&self) -> u32 {
        match self {
            WritingSurface::Vellum { .. } | WritingSurface::Parchment { .. } => 14, // 8-14 days for liming alone
            WritingSurface::Papyrus { .. } => 3,
            WritingSurface::VegetablePapyrus { .. } => 1,
            WritingSurface::LinenPaper { .. } => 7, // Rag rotting + processing
        }
    }
}
```

**Progression:**
- Early game: Vegetable papyrus (peppers, beets) for practice, birch bark as cheap alternative
- Mid game: Parchment from sheepskin/goatskin (pastoral villages)
- Late game: Fine vellum from calfskin, linen rag paper mills

**Gameplay:**
- Players must establish supply chains (raise livestock OR collect rags OR grow papyrus)
- Quality affects ink behavior (feathering on low-quality surfaces)
- Price of books directly tied to writing surface cost
- Monasteries specialize in vellum preparation

### Mechanic 2: Ink Crafting System

**System:** Recipe-based ink creation with ingredient gathering and quality variance

```rust

    Wine(u32),

impl Ink {
    fn permanence_years(&self) -> u32 {
        match self {
            Ink::IronGall { quality, .. } => match quality {
                InkQuality::Crude => 50,
                InkQuality::Standard => 200,
                InkQuality::Fine => 1000,  // Archival!
            },
            Ink::Carbon { .. } => 2000,  // Extremely stable
            Ink::Berry { age_days, .. } => {
                if *age_days > 365 { 5 } else { 10 }  // Fades quickly
            },
            Ink::MineralPigment { .. } => 500,  // Stable but can flake
            Ink::Experimental { stability, .. } => (stability * 100.0) as u32,
        }
    }

    fn gathering_difficulty(&self) -> Difficulty {
        match self {
            Ink::IronGall { .. } => Difficulty::Hard,  // Oak galls seasonal, vitriol requires trade
            Ink::Carbon { .. } => Difficulty::Easy,    // Collect soot from any fire
            Ink::Berry { .. } => Difficulty::Easy,     // Forage wild berries
            Ink::MineralPigment { .. } => Difficulty::Medium,  // Find colored stones, grind
            Ink::Experimental { .. } => Difficulty::Variable,
        }
    }
}

// Special mechanic: Stonemason synergy
impl StoneMason {
    fn produce_stone_dust_byproduct(&mut self, work_hours: u32) -> Ingredient {
        // Stonemasons produce colored stone dust as they carve
        let dust_amount = work_hours / 10;  // 1 unit of dust per 10 hours carving
        let dust_color = self.current_stone_type.natural_color();
        Ingredient::StoneDust(dust_amount)
    }
}
```

**Progression:**
- Early game: Berry inks (foraged), carbon ink from hearth soot
- Mid game: Iron gall ink (requires trade for vitriol OR finding bog iron)
- Late game: Mineral pigment inks for illumination, experimental oil-based inks

**Gameplay:**
- Ink quality affects document longevity (iron gall records last centuries)
- Berry inks cheap for practice, lessons, temporary notes
- Stone dust from stonemasons creates economy loop (masonry → pigment → illumination)
- Iron gall ink corrodes parchment over centuries (historical accuracy!)

```rust
    },
    MedievalCodex {
        raised_cords: u32,         // 3-5 raised cords on spine
        laced_boards: bool,        // Cords laced through wooden boards
    },
    SimpleStitching {
        thread_type: ThreadType,
    },
}

enum CoverMaterial {
    WoodenBoards { wood_type: WoodType, thickness_mm: f32 },
    Leather { leather_type: LeatherType, tooled: bool },
    Fabric { fabric_type: FabricType },
}

impl Book {
    fn from_quires(quires: Vec<Quire>, binder_skill: f32) -> Self {
        let binding_type = if binder_skill > 0.7 {
            BindingType::MedievalCodex {
                raised_cords: 4,
                laced_boards: true,
            }
        } else if binder_skill > 0.4 {
            BindingType::CopticBinding {
                sewing_stations: 3,
                exposed_spine: true,
            }
        } else {
            BindingType::SimpleStitching {
                thread_type: ThreadType::LinenThread,
            }
        };

        let durability = binder_skill * 0.8 + 0.2;  // Min 0.2, max 1.0

        Book {
            quires,
            binding_type,
            cover_material: CoverMaterial::WoodenBoards {
                wood_type: WoodType::Oak,
                thickness_mm: 5.0,
            },
            durability,
            clasps: 2,  // Standard 2 clasps
            illuminated: false,
        }
    }

    fn production_time_hours(&self) -> u32 {
        let quire_time = self.quires.len() as u32 * 2;  // 2 hours per quire to sew
        let binding_time = match &self.binding_type {
            BindingType::CopticBinding { sewing_stations, .. } => sewing_stations * 3,
            BindingType::MedievalCodex { raised_cords, .. } => raised_cords * 4,
            BindingType::SimpleStitching { .. } => 2,
        };
        let cover_time = 6;  // Covering takes significant skill

        quire_time + binding_time + cover_time
    }
}

struct Bindery {
    tools: Vec<BindingTool>,
    thread_stock: u32,
    board_stock: u32,
}

enum BindingTool {
    SewingFrame,     // Holds quires during sewing
    Awl,             // Pierces sewing holes
    BoneFolder,      // Creases folds, burnishes
    Press,           // Flattens and shapes book
    Hammer,          // Sets spine, attaches boards
    KnifePlow,       // Trims page edges
}
```

**Progression:**
- Early game: Simple stitched quires, no covers (pamphlets)
- Mid game: Coptic binding with wooden boards
- Late game: Full codex with raised cords, leather covers, metal clasps

**Gameplay:**
- Binderies in monasteries and university towns
- Book durability affects whether it survives decades/centuries
- Clasps required in humid climates (parchment warps without them)
- Players can commission custom books (prayer books, herbals, grimoires)

### Mechanic 4: Manuscript Illumination

**System:** Multi-stage art process with expensive materials and skill checks

```rust
struct IlluminatedManuscript {
    base_book: Book,
    illuminations: Vec<Illumination>,
    gold_leaf_sheets: u32,
    total_value: u32,        // Can be worth small fortune
}

struct Illumination {
    page_number: u32,
    illumination_type: IlluminationType,
    gold_applied: bool,
    pigments_used: Vec<Pigment>,
    quality: ArtQuality,     // Based on illuminator skill
}

enum IlluminationType {
    HistoriatedInitial { size_mm: u32, scene_complexity: u32 },
    Miniature { width_mm: u32, height_mm: u32 },
    BorderDecoration { sides: Vec<BorderSide> },
    Marginalia { subject: MarginaliaSubject },
}

enum MarginaliaSubject {
    Religious,
    Humorous,        // Knights fighting snails!
    Grotesque,       // Bizarre creatures
    Political,       // Commentary on current events
    Obscene,         // Yes, really - common in medieval manuscripts
}

struct IlluminationProcess {
    stage: IlluminationStage,
    time_remaining_hours: u32,
}

enum IlluminationStage {
    SurfacePreparation,           // Pumice, pouncing
    GessoApplication { armenian_bole: bool },  // Red clay for warmth
    GoldLeafApplication,          // Delicate work
    Burnishing { tool: BurnishingTool },       // Stone or hound's tooth
    PigmentApplication { layers: u32 },
    DetailWork,                   // Fine lines, highlights
}

enum BurnishingTool {
    SmoothStone,
    HoundTooth,      // Historical! Creates brilliant shine
    AgateStone,      // Best quality
}

enum Pigment {
    // Expensive
    LapisLazuli { grams: f32 },   // Ultramarine blue - very expensive!
    Gold,                          // For shell gold (powdered)

    // Moderate cost
    Vermillion,                    // Mercury sulfide red
    Minium,                        // Red lead (miniature derives from this)
    Orpiment,                      // Arsenic yellow (toxic!)
    Verdigris,                     // Copper green (corrosive)

    // Cheap earth pigments
    RedOchre,
    YellowOchre,
    Umber,

    // Organic
    MadderRed,                     // From plant root
    IndigoBlue,                    // From plant
    SaffronYellow,                 // From crocus (expensive)
}

impl Pigment {
    fn cost_per_gram(&self) -> u32 {
        match self {
            Pigment::LapisLazuli { .. } => 100,  // Imported from Afghanistan!
            Pigment::Gold => 200,
            Pigment::Vermillion => 20,
            Pigment::Minium => 10,
            Pigment::Orpiment => 15,
            Pigment::Verdigris => 5,
            Pigment::RedOchre | Pigment::YellowOchre | Pigment::Umber => 1,
            Pigment::MadderRed => 8,
            Pigment::IndigoBlue => 12,
            Pigment::SaffronYellow => 50,
        }
    }

    fn is_toxic(&self) -> bool {
        matches!(self, Pigment::Orpiment | Pigment::Vermillion | Pigment::Minium)
    }

    fn is_corrosive(&self) -> bool {
        matches!(self, Pigment::Verdigris)
    }
}

impl IlluminatedManuscript {
    fn calculate_value(&self) -> u32 {
        let base_book_value = 50;  // Standard codex
        let gold_value = self.gold_leaf_sheets * 20;  // Gold expensive
        let pigment_value: u32 = self.illuminations.iter()
            .flat_map(|i| &i.pigments_used)
            .map(|p| p.cost_per_gram())
            .sum();
        let labor_value = self.illuminations.len() as u32 * 30;  // Skilled labor costly
        let quality_multiplier = self.average_quality();

        ((base_book_value + gold_value + pigment_value + labor_value) as f32 * quality_multiplier) as u32
    }

    fn production_time_weeks(&self) -> u32 {
        let illumination_hours: u32 = self.illuminations.iter()
            .map(|i| match &i.illumination_type {
                IlluminationType::HistoriatedInitial { scene_complexity, .. } => scene_complexity * 8,
                IlluminationType::Miniature { .. } => 20,
                IlluminationType::BorderDecoration { .. } => 6,
                IlluminationType::Marginalia { .. } => 2,
            })
            .sum();

        (illumination_hours / 40) + 1  // 40-hour work weeks, round up
    }
}
```

**Progression:**
- Early game: Simple red/black initials (cheap pigments)
- Mid game: Border decorations with earth pigments
- Late game: Full historiated initials, gold leaf, lapis lazuli ultramarine

**Gameplay:**
- Illuminated manuscripts are luxury goods (worth fortunes)
- Monasteries and wealthy patrons commission illuminated works
- Toxic pigments (orpiment, minium) have health risks for illuminators
- Lapis lazuli requires long-distance trade from Afghanistan
- Players can add humorous marginalia (easter eggs in books!)

## 6. Implementation Notes

### 6.1 Profession Specializations

**Parchment Maker**
- Requires access to livestock (calf/sheep/goat)
- 14-day production cycle
- Tools: Scudder, lunar knife, stretching frame, pumice
- Can supply scribes and bookbinders

**Vatman (Paper Maker)**
- Requires water mill (for stamping mill)
- Rag collection network
- Tools: Vat, mould, deckle, press
- Can produce paper cheaper than parchment (late game)

**Ink Maker**
- Foraging for berries, oak galls
- Trade connections for vitriol (ferrous sulphate)
- Mineral prospecting for ochre/umber deposits
- Can supply scribes

**Scribe**
- Literacy required (learned profession)
- Produces documents, copies books
- Consumes ink and writing surface
- Revenue from copying fees

**Bookbinder**
- Requires carpentry (wooden boards) and sewing skills
- Tools: Sewing frame, awl, press, bone folder
- Adds value to loose quires
- Can repair damaged books

**Illuminator**
- Advanced artistic skill
- Access to expensive pigments (lapis lazuli!)
- Gold leaf application technique
- Creates luxury manuscripts worth fortunes

### 6.2 Economic Loops

**Livestock → Parchment → Books**
- Raise calves/sheep → Slaughter → Parchment maker → Scribe → Bookbinder

**Textiles → Paper → Books**
- Old clothing → Rag collector → Vatman → Scribe → Bookbinder

**Foraging → Ink → Documents**
- Berries/Oak galls → Ink maker → Scribe

**Mining → Pigments → Illumination**
- Ochre deposits → Grind minerals → Illuminator → Luxury manuscripts

**Masonry → Pigments → Art**
- Stonemason carving → Colored stone dust byproduct → Illuminator

### 6.3 Historical Accuracy Notes

**Parchment Dominance**: In medieval Europe, parchment/vellum dominated until paper became widespread in 14th-15th centuries. Paper was viewed as less permanent and not suitable for important documents.

**Iron Gall Permanence**: The permanence of iron gall ink made it essential for legal documents, religious texts, and archival records. The fact that it eventually corrodes the substrate is historically accurate.

**Illumination Costs**: A single illuminated manuscript could cost as much as a house or small farm. Lapis lazuli for ultramarine blue was literally worth its weight in gold, imported from Afghanistan.

**Vegetable Papyrus**: While not historically used in medieval Europe, the technique works and provides an interesting experimental/discovery path for players. The user's insight about peppers and other vegetables is chemically sound!

**Berry Ink Fading**: Berry inks were indeed used but primarily for practice, temporary notes, and by those who couldn't afford iron gall ink. The fading over time is historically accurate.

**Stone Dust for Pigments**: Stonemasons absolutely would have had access to colorful stone dust as a byproduct, creating a natural economic synergy with illuminators.
