## Purpose

Four threads left open by the doctrine/sacraments doc: the relic-and-saint economy (legitimacy markets), monastic orders as proto-multinationals (the most durable org forms ever built), councils as constitutional machinery (what happens when the institution deadlocks), and the suppression of mystics (the license-to-prophesy gate — who may claim direct access to the sacred).

## Part 1: The Relic & Saint Economy — Legitimacy Markets

- **Canonization as brand management**: the Church gradually centralized saint-making (local cults → episcopal approval → papal canonization from 993 onward, formalized 1234) — turning a bottom-up popular practice (saints began as *local* acclamations) into a centrally licensed product. The institution took over the production of its own legitimacy objects.
- **The relic market**: saints' remains as the era's ultimate collateral. Louis IX paid 135,000 livres for the Crown of Thorns (1239) — *more than the cost of building Sainte-Chapelle to house it*. Relics conferred legitimacy: altars required them, oaths were sworn on them, pilgrimage routes were built around them.
- **Furta sacra — "sacred thefts"**: relics were systematically *stolen* between cities/monasteries, with the thefts rationalized as the saint choosing to move (Venice's acquisition of St. Mark's body from Alexandria — the foundational relic-heist: the whole city's identity built on stolen bones). Relic acquisition as state/civic soft-power competition.
- **The critique that fueled Reformation**: relic inflation became absurdity — Calvin's inventory joke (enough True Cross wood for a ship; multiple heads of John the Baptist). Protestant iconoclasm was partly a *legitimacy-market crash*: denouncing the assets as counterfeit destroyed their value — and the pilgrimage/dues economy with them.
- **Pilgrimage economy**: routes (Santiago, Canterbury, Rome) as revenue + identity infrastructure; the medieval tourism industry attached to the relic network. Indulgences monetized the same network directly (the trigger for Luther).

## Part 2: Monastic Orders — The Proto-Multinational

The most durable organizational form in European history:

- **The Rule of St. Benedict (c. 530)**: an *org charter* still in use 1,500 years later — abbot election for life, chapter governance, stability vow (the anti-attrition device: monks belong to a house, not a movement), ora et labora as balanced work design. The Rule's genius: it encodes governance so completely that a house can reboot anywhere.
- **Cistercians (12th c.)**: ~750 abbeys at peak, run like a multinational — standardized procedures, lay-brother labor force (conversi — a two-tier membership economy), granges as managed production units, wool trade at scale, annual General Chapter as corporate governance. Franchised expansion: a mother house founds daughters under uniform rules.
- **The accumulation trap**: orders legally couldn't die — property accreted for centuries (estimates: the Church held a third of usable land in parts of Europe). Accumulation → criticism ("the monks own the world") → suppression waves (Hus site confiscations, Henry VIII's dissolution, Josephinism, French nationalization, German mediatization). **Durable institution → asset accumulation → expropriation cycle**: the same property that makes an order immortal makes it a target.
- **Carthusians**: the opposite model — eremitical cells in strict uniformity; proved you can standardize *austerity* (their brand was refusing the accumulation the others took).
- **Jesuit suppression/restoration (1773/1814)**: even the Church's own strike force got sacrificed to state pressure — and regenerated 41 years later through its alumni/school network. **Killable order, unkillable network.**

## Part 3: Councils — Constitutional Machinery for Deadlock

- **Nicaea (325)**: imperial convocation — Constantine called it, presided (unbaptized!), enforced the output; the emperor as convenor/enforcer but *not* theologian. Model: when the org can't self-resolve, the sovereign arbitrates — and charges for it (the faith-power interface in operation).
- **Constance (1414–18)**: the deepest case — THREE rival popes simultaneously (Rome, Avignon, Pisa), the institution's legitimacy system broken. The council deposed/retired all three and elected Martin V. **Conciliarism**: the claim that the council outranks the pope — medieval constitutionalism; the church's own supreme court solving succession crisis that papalism couldn't. (Also the same council that burned Hus under safe-conduct — the arbitration body was also an enforcement body.)
- **Trent (1545–63)**: 18 years, ~25 sessions — counter-doctrine mass-production in response to Protestantism: defined canon, sacraments, justification, seminaries (the institutional answer to Protestant critique: *train the clergy* — education as reform). The Counter-Reformation was substantially built at this one conference-table.
- **Vatican II (1962–65)**: reform via council — aggiornamento ("bringing up to date"): vernacular liturgy, lay roles, ecumenism — an institution deliberately loosening its own gatekeeping to survive competition. Proof the mechanism runs both directions (tighten at Trent, loosen at Vatican II).
- **The pattern**: councils are the *only* override mechanism when the hierarchy deadlocks — the constitutional convention of religious institutions. High-cost, rarely called, capable of anything from burning Hus to electing popes to rewriting the liturgy.

## Part 4: The Suppression of Mystics — The License-to-Prophesy Gate

The recurring fight: **who may claim direct access to the sacred?**

- **Marguerite Porete (burned 1310, Paris)**: wrote *The Mirror of Simple Souls* — taught that the annihilated soul needs neither virtues nor the Church's intermediation. Refused to recant or even speak at her trial; the inquisitors' dilemma: her serenity itself looked like her proof. The book survived her — copied secretly for centuries under other names (the text the institution couldn't kill).
- **Meister Eckhart (condemned posthumously 1329)**: Dominican master preaching the Godhead beyond God — his case showed the boundary: speculation within the order's language was tolerated; Porete's vernacular, woman-authored, unsupervised text was not. The offense wasn't heresy of content as much as **heresy of access** — claiming the sacred without license.
- **The beguines suppression**: semi-religious women's communities — lived in towns, no permanent vows, no male supervision, supported themselves. Suppressed at Vienne (1311–12) as "enclosure without the rule" — the institution couldn't classify them, so it eliminated the category. Unclassified female religious autonomy was intolerable specifically *because* it had no gatekeeper.
- **Joan of Arc (1431)**: mystic → military commander → martyr → saint (canonized 1920 — the 500-year redemption arc). Her trial is the best-documented inquisition interrogation: the examiners kept trying to classify her (heretic? witch? schismatic? cross-dresser — the male clothing charge is what they finally used as relapse-proof) — the record shows an institution that could neither believe nor disbelieve her without losing control of the category of revelation itself.
- **The two-paths rule**: mystics who *submitted* experience to doctrine were canonized (Julian of Norwich, Catherine of Siena — who advised and reproached popes but stayed inside the frame); mystics who claimed experience *sufficed* were eliminated (Porete, Eckhart, the beguines). The license-to-prophesy gate is the exact inverse of the prophetic-mobilization mechanic — the institution's counter-move against charismatic competition.

## Cross-Cutting Patterns

1. **Legitimacy is a manufactured market**: relics, canonization, pilgrimage — the institution doesn't just hold legitimacy, it *produces* the objects that carry it (and inflation/counterfeit-crash dynamics apply — Reformation iconoclasm was a legitimacy-market crash)
2. **Charter durability**: the Rule of Benedict is proof that a well-designed org charter outlives empires — but accumulated assets make the immortal organization a target (durability → wealth → expropriation)
3. **Deadlock resolution exists**: when the institution's own machinery fails (three popes), a higher-order mechanism (council) overrides — constitutional machinery is load-bearing exactly when everything else breaks
4. **Access-control is the deepest fight**: the mystics' suppression is the same fight as scripture-vernacularization (Lollards), ordination monopoly, and the Inquisition's dossiers — *who may speak for the sacred*. Gatekeeping is the institution's irreducible function, and every counter-movement's first target.

## Game Mechanic Proposals

### Mechanic 1: Relic/cult economy
`relics` as legitimacy objects: factions acquire them (purchase, theft, donation, manufacture — counterfeit risk!), each carries `prestige` + `pilgrimage_revenue` + `legitimacy`. Furta sacra events: rival factions can steal them; the story of the theft *adds* prestige if spun right. Crash events: iconoclast/counterfeit exposé crashes the whole market (all factions' relic value drops — the Reformation scenario).

### Mechanic 2: Order-type organizations
`religious_order` org type with charter rules: stability (no member attrition), standardized procedures (uniform ops across sites), lay-brother labor economy (two-tier membership), daughter-house franchising (found new houses under uniform charter). The trade: extreme durability + steady accumulation → mounting `expropriation_risk` (the richer the order, the more appetizing to cash-strapped power).

### Mechanic 3: Council override events
When a religious institution deadlocks (succession crisis, doctrinal schism), a `council` event unlocks: factions present positions, resolve by influence/negotiation → binding output. Cost: enormous legitimacy spend; the sovereign who convenes gains leverage over the church (the Constantine pattern — convening is itself a power move).

### Mechanic 4: The license-to-prophesy gate
`revelation` events spawn mystic NPCs: institution must adjudicate — **canonize** (absorb the charisma, gain legitimacy + a saint asset), **license** (tolerate with conditions), or **suppress** (remove; risk martyrdom-conversion making them bigger dead than alive — Joan, Hus). The Porete/Eckhart rule: suppressing *access claims* is cheap; suppressing *content heresy* is expensive; the most dangerous mystic is the one who doesn't need you.

### Mechanic 5: Furta sacra / legitimacy-object raids
Movements/factions can raid legitimacy objects (relic theft, archive seizure, shrine occupation) — transferring the object's prestige to the captor if the transfer story holds (the saint consented, the regime was illegitimate). Medieval version of the information-war mechanic: steal the symbols, not just the territory.
